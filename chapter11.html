Chapter 11 — Plateaus & Plains

Physical Geography • UPSC CSE Prelims + Mains

Plateaus: Meaning, Characteristics and Formation

A plateau is an extensive area of relatively elevated land with a generally flat or gently undulating surface. Unlike mountains, plateaus usually have broad upland surfaces rather than sharp peaks.

  • Elevated terrain: Higher than the surrounding lowlands.
  • Flat or gently sloping top: Although erosion may make parts highly dissected.
  • Steep margins/escarpments: Many plateaus have abrupt edges.
  • Variable size: From small uplands to vast continental plateaus.
  • Geological diversity: May develop through tectonic uplift, lava accumulation, erosion or sedimentary processes.
Uplift / lava accumulation / denudation → elevated surface → long-term erosion → valleys, canyons, escarpments and other plateau landforms

Examples: Tibetan Plateau, Deccan Plateau, Colorado Plateau, Brazilian Highlands, Ethiopian Highlands.

Major Types of Plateaus

Type Location/Formation Examples
Intermontane Plateau Located between or surrounded by mountain ranges; generally associated with major tectonic uplift. Tibetan Plateau, Altiplano, Anatolian Plateau
Piedmont Plateau Located at the foothills/base of mountains; transitional between mountains and adjacent lowlands. Piedmont areas of the Appalachians; Piedmont region of Italy
Continental Plateau Large elevated areas covering extensive portions of continents, often bounded by lower terrain/coasts. Peninsular/Deccan Plateau, Antarctic Plateau
Volcanic Plateau Built by repeated outpourings of highly fluid lava over extensive areas. Deccan Plateau, Columbia Plateau
Dissected Plateau Plateau deeply cut by rivers and streams over long periods. Colorado Plateau and many strongly eroded uplands
UPSC distinction: A plateau may be formed by uplift, but its present landscape may be strongly modified by erosion/dissection. “Plateau” therefore describes its broad topographic form, not necessarily one single mode of origin.

Intermontane Plateaus

These occur between mountain systems and are commonly associated with tectonic uplift and mountain building.

Tibetan Plateau: bounded broadly by the Himalayas to the south and the Kunlun–Tian Shan systems to the north/northwest. It is the world's largest and highest extensive plateau.

Indian Plate–Eurasian Plate convergence → crustal shortening/thickening → uplift → Tibetan Plateau

The plateau strongly influences:

  • Asian monsoon circulation
  • Regional temperature patterns
  • Atmospheric circulation and heating
  • Glacial and river systems

Piedmont Plateaus

Develop near the foot of mountain systems and form a transition between mountains and plains.

They may develop through deposition, uplift and erosion around mountain fronts.

Examples: Piedmont region of the Appalachian Mountains; piedmont areas around the Alps.

Dissected Plateaus

These are plateaus heavily cut by rivers, streams and erosion, producing a rugged landscape of deep valleys, ravines and gorges.

Plateau uplift → prolonged river erosion → valley incision → rugged/dissected plateau

Examples: Colorado Plateau, parts of the Appalachian Plateau.

Volcanic Plateaus

Form through repeated outpourings of relatively fluid lava over large areas.

Major fissure eruptions → enormous lava flows → repeated lava sheets → cooling/solidification → extensive volcanic plateau

Deccan Plateau: associated with extensive basaltic lava flows and the Deccan Traps.

Columbia Plateau: major flood-basalt province in the northwestern United States.

Why plateau soils can be fertile: weathering of basalt can produce mineral-rich soils; the Deccan region is particularly associated with black cotton soil (regur).

Continental Plateaus and Major World Plateaus

Continental plateaus are broad elevated surfaces developed through large-scale crustal uplift, volcanic accumulation or long-term erosion.

Plateau Region Important significance
Tibetan Plateau China–India region Highest extensive plateau; major climatic influence; source region of several major Asian rivers.
Deccan Plateau India Basaltic terrain; Deccan Traps; black soil; major mineral and agricultural region.
Colorado Plateau USA Highly dissected landscape with canyons and mesas.
Columbia Plateau USA Flood-basalt volcanic plateau.
Ethiopian Highlands Ethiopia Highland terrain; major river-source region; often called the “Roof of Africa”.
Brazilian Highlands Brazil Extensive uplands; influence drainage, climate and resources.
Patagonian Plateau Argentina–Chile Elevated, relatively dry landscape east of the Andes.
Altiplano Bolivia–Peru High intermontane plateau of the Andes.

Significance of Plateaus

Dimension Importance
Mineral resources Plateau regions often expose ancient rocks and contain iron ore, coal, manganese, gold and other minerals.
Agriculture Suitable areas support agriculture depending on soil, drainage and climate; basaltic plateaus may support highly fertile soils.
Hydropower Steep escarpments, gorges and river valleys provide sites for hydroelectric generation.
Forestry and biodiversity Elevation and climatic variation create distinct ecological zones.
Tourism Canyons, waterfalls, escarpments and distinctive geology create major tourist landscapes.
Settlement and transport Gentle upland surfaces may support settlement, but steep escarpments and dissected terrain can limit infrastructure.
Climate Higher elevation generally produces lower temperatures; large plateaus can influence regional atmospheric circulation.
Mains linkage: Plateau → geology → soils → minerals → drainage → agriculture → settlement → industry → regional development.

Plains: Meaning, Characteristics and Formation

A plain is a broad area of relatively low-relief land with little variation in elevation.

  • Generally flat or gently undulating.
  • Usually have relatively low slopes.
  • Often contain deep and fertile soils.
  • Favour agriculture, transport, urbanisation and dense settlement.
  • May develop through deposition, erosion, structural processes or marine processes.

Major Types of Plains

Type Formation Examples
Structural Plains Develop on relatively horizontal or gently inclined rock structures with limited deformation. Parts of the Interior Plains of North America; East European Plain
Erosional Plains Produced by prolonged erosion and denudation of former uplands. Peneplains; landscapes approaching a low-relief surface through long-term denudation
Depositional Plains Built through accumulation of sediments transported by rivers, glaciers, wind or marine processes. Indo-Gangetic Plain, Mississippi Plain, Ganga-Brahmaputra Plain
Abyssal Plains Very flat deep-ocean-floor surfaces formed by accumulation of fine sediments. Abyssal plains of Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Oceans
Important clarification: Individual plains may have more than one process involved in their evolution. Classification depends on the dominant formative mechanism and textbook convention.

Structural Plains

These develop where relatively resistant strata remain broadly horizontal or gently tilted.

Because the geological structure is relatively stable and horizontal, extensive low-relief surfaces can develop.

Examples: East European Plain and parts of the Interior Plains of North America.

Erosional Plains

Develop through prolonged removal of material from previously elevated terrain.

Uplifted land → prolonged weathering + erosion → reduction of relief → low-relief erosional surface

Peneplain: theoretical/ideal low-relief surface produced by prolonged denudation, approaching base level.

UPSC concept: A peneplain is an erosional concept, not simply any flat plain.

Depositional Plains

These are formed through the accumulation of sediments transported by geomorphic agents.

Erosion → transport → decrease in energy/carrying capacity → sediment accumulation → depositional plain

Major examples include:

  • Alluvial plains – river deposition
  • Floodplains – repeated flood deposition
  • Deltaic plains – deposition near river mouths
  • Glacial plains – glacial and meltwater deposits
  • Loess plains – wind-blown silt accumulation

Major Depositional Plains and Fluvial Features

Alluvial Plains

Alluvial plains are built mainly from sediments deposited by rivers over long periods.

They commonly contain sand, silt, clay and gravel, with fertile soils in many regions.

Example: Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra Plain.

Mountain erosion → sediment carried by rivers → deposition in low-gradient basin → alluvial plain formation

Floodplains

A floodplain is a relatively flat area adjacent to a river that is periodically inundated and receives flood-borne sediment.

Important features include:

  • Natural levees
  • Backswamps
  • Oxbow lakes
  • Meander scars
  • Point bars

Alluvial Fans and Piedmont Zones

Mountain front → abrupt decrease in gradient → reduced river velocity → sediment deposition → alluvial fan/cone → piedmont plain

Bhabar: coarse, porous piedmont belt along the Himalayan foothills where streams may disappear underground.

Terai: belt south of the Bhabar where groundwater re-emerges, producing wetter conditions and marshy terrain in many areas.

UPSC linkage: Bhabar and Terai illustrate how sediment size + slope + infiltration + groundwater emergence control landscape and drainage.

Deltaic Plains

Deltaic plains develop near river mouths where sediment deposition dominates over removal by marine processes.

Examples: Ganga-Brahmaputra, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Nile deltas.

They are commonly characterised by:

  • Distributary channels
  • Natural levees
  • Mudflats
  • Marshes/wetlands
  • High agricultural productivity
  • Flood and cyclone vulnerability

Abyssal Plains

Abyssal plains are among the flattest surfaces on Earth and occur on the deep ocean floor, generally at depths of roughly 4,000–6,000 m.

They develop mainly through the accumulation of fine sediments that bury irregularities of the oceanic crust.

Oceanic crust irregularities → long-term sediment accumulation → uneven surface progressively buried → extremely flat abyssal plain

They are especially extensive in parts of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans and occur in other ocean basins as well.

Plateaus vs Plains

Feature Plateau Plain
Elevation Relatively high. Generally low or relatively level.
Relief Often bounded by escarpments and may be strongly dissected. Generally low relief.
Main formation Uplift, volcanism, erosion and geological processes. Deposition, erosion and structural processes.
Drainage Often deeply incised due to elevation and gradient. Usually lower-gradient and more meandering in major alluvial plains.
Agriculture Depends strongly on soil, slope and water availability. Often highly favourable due to flat terrain and fertile soils.
Settlement Variable; fragmented terrain can constrain connectivity. Generally favours dense settlements and transport networks.

Geomorphic and Human Significance

Plateaus and plains represent two contrasting but interconnected components of the landscape.

Plateau/highland → erosion → sediment production → river transport → plain/basin deposition

This creates strong source-to-sink linkages between uplands and lowlands.

Dimension Plateaus Plains
Resources Minerals, forests, hydropower. Fertile soils, groundwater, construction materials.
Agriculture Depends on soil and water; black-soil regions can be highly productive. Often highly productive alluvial agricultural belts.
Industry Mineral-based industries often develop near ore deposits. Agriculture-based and market-oriented industries often concentrate in accessible lowlands.
Transport Escarpments and dissected terrain can raise infrastructure costs. Low relief generally facilitates roads, railways and urban expansion.
Hazards Erosion, landslides, water scarcity in some areas. Flooding, riverbank erosion, waterlogging and delta/coastal hazards.
Ecology Distinct altitude-driven ecosystems. Wetlands, floodplains, grasslands and intensive agricultural ecosystems.

Common Prelims Traps

Trap Correct concept
All plateaus are volcanic. Plateaus have diverse origins: tectonic uplift, volcanism, erosion and other geological processes.
Tibetan Plateau is a volcanic plateau. It is primarily a major intermontane/tectonic plateau.
Deccan Plateau is an ordinary erosional plateau. Much of the Deccan is strongly associated with flood-basalt volcanism.
Piedmont means an intermontane basin. Piedmont = at the foot of mountains.
A dissected plateau has always been formed by rivers alone. Its present dissection is commonly the result of prolonged erosion, but the plateau may have another original mode of formation.
All plains are depositional. Plains may be structural, erosional or depositional.
Peneplain means any flat depositional plain. Peneplain is an erosional concept referring to a low-relief surface formed through prolonged denudation.
Abyssal plains are continental plains. They are found on the deep ocean floor.
Alluvial plains form mainly through wind deposition. Alluvium is transported and deposited mainly by running water.
Natural levees are artificial embankments. They are natural depositional ridges along river banks.
Plains always have no erosion. Erosion and deposition can occur simultaneously; the defining characteristic is generally low relief.
Plateaus are always unsuitable for agriculture. Agricultural suitability depends on soil, rainfall, irrigation, slope and drainage.
Black soil is associated with every plateau. Black cotton soil is especially associated with basaltic Deccan terrain.
A plateau must have a perfectly flat top. Many plateaus are dissected and strongly eroded.

Short Revision Sheet

Plateau = elevated + relatively flat/gently rolling upland.

Plateau → uplift / lava / erosion → elevated surface → dissection by erosion

Main plateau types: Intermontane + Piedmont + Continental + Volcanic + Dissected.

Intermontane → between mountains

Piedmont → at mountain foot

Volcanic → extensive lava flows

Dissected → deeply cut by erosion

Deccan → basalt → Deccan Traps → weathering → black soil → cotton/agriculture
Indian–Eurasian convergence → crustal thickening/uplift → Tibetan Plateau → climate/monsoon influence

Plain = broad, low-relief, flat or gently undulating surface.

Three major genetic categories: Structural + Erosional + Depositional.

Erosion → transport → deposition → alluvial plain

Alluvial plain: river-borne sediments.

Floodplain: periodically flooded river-side depositional surface.

Deltaic plain: sediment accumulation near river mouth.

Abyssal plain: very flat deep-ocean floor.

Mountain erosion → river transport → basin deposition → plains

Bhabar: coarse, porous piedmont belt → streams infiltrate.

Terai: groundwater re-emerges south of Bhabar → wetter conditions.

Plateau → minerals + hydropower + forests
Plains → fertile soils + water + transport → dense settlement + agriculture + urbanisation

Master linkage for Mains:

Relief → Geology → Soil → Drainage → Resources → Agriculture → Settlement → Economy → Hazards
