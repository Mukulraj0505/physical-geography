Geomorphic Processes: Basic Framework

Geomorphic processes are the natural processes responsible for creating, modifying and destroying Earth's landforms.

Geomorphic agents are the natural forces/media through which these processes operate, such as water, wind, ice, waves, gravity and living organisms.

Geomorphic processes → Endogenic + Exogenic
Endogenic Exogenic
Originate from Earth's internal energy. Operate mainly through surface/external energy.
Build relief or deform Earth's crust. Weather, erode, transport and deposit material.
Examples: diastrophism, earthquakes, volcanism. Examples: weathering, erosion, transportation, deposition.
Endogenic → Diastrophic + Sudden
Exogenic → Weathering + Erosion + Transportation + Deposition

Endogenic forces are ultimately powered mainly by Earth's internal heat, derived from primordial heat and radioactive decay, while exogenic processes are powered largely by solar energy + gravity.

Earth's Internal Dynamics

Internal heat → temperature/density differences → mantle movement → plate motion → deformation of crust → mountains + rifts + faults + earthquakes + volcanism

Plate tectonics is the central framework connecting many endogenic geomorphic processes.

Diastrophism: Slow Crustal Movements

Diastrophic forces are slow, large-scale movements and deformation of Earth's crust caused mainly by tectonic processes.

They are divided broadly into:

Diastrophism → Epeirogenic movements + Orogenic movements

Epeirogenic Movements

Epeirogenic movements are predominantly broad, regional vertical movements of the crust involving uplift or subsidence, generally producing relatively little folding compared with orogenic deformation.

Vertical crustal movement → uplift → emergence
Vertical crustal movement → subsidence → submergence
  • Can affect very large continental areas or specific coastal regions.
  • May alter relative land–sea levels without producing intense folding.
  • Can contribute to emergence of land and formation of extensive plateaus.

Examples: uplift of the Colorado Plateau; broad uplift/subsidence associated with continental areas.

Orogenic Movements

Orogeny refers to mountain-building processes involving significant horizontal compression, extension, folding, faulting and crustal deformation.

Plate convergence → compression → folding + thrust faulting + crustal shortening → mountain building

Extension can also create tectonic relief through faulting and crustal stretching.

Faults and Tectonic Fractures

A fault is a fracture in Earth's crust along which there has been significant displacement of rocks.

Term Meaning
Fault plane Surface along which displacement takes place.
Fault dip Angle made by the fault plane with the horizontal.
Hanging wall Block of rock lying above an inclined fault plane.
Footwall Block lying below the fault plane.
Fault scarp Steep topographic step created by fault displacement.

Types of Faults

Fault Dominant stress Movement Typical setting
Normal fault Tension Hanging wall moves downward relative to footwall. Divergent settings, rift zones
Reverse fault Compression Hanging wall moves upward relative to footwall. Compressional settings
Thrust fault Strong compression Low-angle reverse fault. Mountain belts and convergent boundaries
Strike-slip fault Shear Blocks move mainly horizontally past one another. Transform boundaries
Mnemonic:
Normal = apart → wall goes down
Reverse = together → wall goes up
Strike-slip = slide sideways

Fault Systems

A fault system is a group of related faults produced by a common tectonic stress regime.

Multiple normal faults can produce characteristic block landscapes.

Normal faulting → blocks move vertically → uplifted block = Horst → down-dropped block = Graben

Horst and Graben

Horst Graben
Uplifted crustal block between faults. Down-dropped crustal block between faults.
May form elongated block mountains. May form elongated structural valleys.
Example: Vosges / Black Forest region. Example: Rhine Graben, parts of East African Rift.
Two normal faults + central block rises → Horst
Two normal faults + central block subsides → Graben / Rift Valley floor

Important distinction: A rift valley is commonly associated with extension + normal faulting + down-dropped blocks.

Step Faults

Step faulting occurs where a series of roughly parallel normal faults produce blocks at progressively different elevations.

Repeated normal faults → successive downthrows → stair-step topography → step-fault landscape

Examples: parts of the East African Rift and Western Ghats-related faulted terrains.

Rift Valleys and Continental Rifting

A rift valley is a long depression associated mainly with crustal extension and normal faulting.

Plate divergence → crust stretches → normal faults develop → central block subsides → rift valley

East African Rift System

The East African Rift System represents an active continental-rifting environment. The African Plate is being progressively divided into the Nubian and Somali plates, with the region involving several interacting fault and volcanic systems.

African continental crust → extension → faulting + volcanism → East African Rift → progressive thinning → future oceanic opening possible
  • The rift extends through parts of Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Malawi and Mozambique.
  • It includes major rift valleys, volcanic zones and deep lakes.
  • The process is extremely slow and would take millions of years to produce a mature new ocean basin.
  • The Afar region is especially important because continental rifting approaches a transition towards oceanic spreading.
Prelims trap: Africa has not already split into two continents. It is undergoing active continental rifting that may eventually lead to a new ocean basin.

Folds and Orogenic Structures

A fold is a bend or warp in rock strata produced mainly by tectonic stress, especially compression.

Compression → rock layers shorten → layers bend → folds develop → fold mountains may form on a large scale

Parts of a Fold

Term Meaning
Limb Side of a fold.
Hinge Zone where curvature is greatest.
Axial plane Imaginary plane dividing the fold as symmetrically as possible and containing the hinge/axial region.
Hinge line / fold axis Line following the zone of maximum curvature along a fold.
Fold amplitude Vertical measure of the displacement from the median level of the folded layer toward crest/trough.
Wavelength Horizontal distance between successive corresponding crests or troughs.
Anticline Arch-like upward fold; in a normal anticline the oldest rocks are generally exposed near the core.
Syncline Trough-like downward fold; in a normal syncline the youngest rocks are generally near the core.
Mnemonic:
Anti = Arch / Age-old at core
Syn = Sink / Young at core

Major Types of Folds

Type Characteristic Example / significance
Symmetrical Both limbs have similar inclination. Relatively balanced compressional deformation.
Asymmetrical Limbs have unequal inclination. Common in stronger or uneven compression.
Monoclinal One limb bends downward/upward between relatively flatter layers. Associated with underlying structural movement.
Isoclinal Limbs are nearly parallel. Strong compression.
Overturned One limb has been pushed beyond the vertical so both limbs dip in the same general direction. Strong deformation.
Recumbent Fold becomes nearly horizontal; axial plane is highly inclined or nearly horizontal. Intense compressional deformation.
Chevron Sharp angular hinges with relatively straight limbs. Common in strongly layered rocks under compression.
Nappe Large sheet of rock displaced considerable distances along a low-angle thrust. Important in major mountain belts such as the Alps and Himalaya.

Mountains: Formation and Major Types

Mountains develop through several processes. They should therefore not be treated as if all mountains were formed by folding alone.

Type Main process Examples
Fold Mountains Compression, folding and crustal shortening, generally at convergent plate boundaries. Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies
Block Mountains Faulting and uplift/downthrow of crustal blocks. Vosges, Black Forest, Sierra Nevada
Dome Mountains Upwarping caused by magmatic intrusion or broad crustal uplift. Black Hills, USA
Volcanic Mountains Accumulation of lava, ash and pyroclastic material around volcanic vents. Mount Fuji, Mount St. Helens
Relic / Residual Mountains Resistant remnants left after prolonged erosion and denudation. Aravallis, Eastern Ghats
Correction: Fold, block, dome, volcanic and relic/residual mountains are different genetic categories. They should not all be described as direct products of “orogenic forces.”

Fold Mountains

Convergence → compression → folding + thrusting → crustal shortening + thickening → uplift → fold mountain system

The world's great young fold mountains occur prominently along active convergent plate margins.

Examples: Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies.

UPSC 2014 Mains: UPSC directly asked why fold mountain systems are located along continental margins and their relationship with earthquakes and volcanoes. Hence the combination fold mountains + convergent boundaries + seismicity + volcanism is a major Mains theme.

Himalayas

Indian Plate → northward convergence → collision with Eurasian Plate → crustal shortening + folding + thrusting → Himalayan uplift

The Himalayas remain tectonically active and are therefore associated with earthquakes, landslides and continuing uplift.

Block Mountains

Block mountains form mainly through large-scale faulting.

Tensional forces → normal faults → crustal blocks displaced → uplifted block = horst → block mountain

Examples: Vosges and Black Forest in Europe; Sierra Nevada in the United States.

Prelims 2024: UPSC tested the distinction among Vosges, Alps, Appalachians and Andes, including fold vs block mountain classification.

Dome Mountains

Dome mountains are rounded or elliptical uplifts produced by broad upwarping, commonly associated with magmatic intrusions or tectonic uplift.

Magma intrusion / crustal uplift → upward warping → erosion strips upper layers → resistant rocks remain → dome mountain

Example: Black Hills, South Dakota, USA.

Volcanic Mountains

Magma rises → reaches surface → lava + ash + pyroclasts accumulate → repeated eruptions → volcanic cone/mountain

Examples: Mount Fuji, Mount St. Helens.

Volcanic mountains are commonly associated with convergent subduction zones or hotspots, though their exact tectonic setting varies.

Residual / Relic Mountains

Old mountain/plateau → prolonged weathering + erosion → weaker rocks removed → resistant remnants remain → residual mountain

Examples: Aravalli Range and parts of the Eastern Ghats.

Sudden Endogenic Forces

Sudden forces act rapidly and can produce major changes in Earth's surface over seconds to minutes.

Main examples:

Sudden endogenic forces → Earthquakes + Volcanic eruptions

Earthquakes

Stress accumulation along fault → rupture/slip → sudden elastic energy release → seismic waves → earthquake

The same basic process is explained by the Elastic Rebound Theory.

Most earthquakes are associated with tectonic plate movement, especially at plate boundaries.

Plate motion → friction/locking → stress accumulation → sudden slip → seismic waves

Volcanic Activity

Magma generation → magma rises → pressure/ buoyancy drive ascent → eruption → lava + ash + gases → volcanic landforms

Volcanism can produce volcanic cones, lava plateaus, calderas, lava flows and ash deposits.

Example: Deccan Traps are ancient large-scale flood-basalt formations, while Mount St. Helens represents explosive stratovolcanic activity.

Exogenic Forces

Exogenic forces operate at or near Earth's surface and are responsible for weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition.

Weathering → breakdown of rock → erosion → removal → transportation → deposition → new landform
Process Meaning Major agents
Weathering In-situ breakdown or decomposition of rocks. Temperature change, water, chemicals, organisms
Erosion Detachment and removal of weathered/rock material. Running water, wind, glaciers, waves
Transportation Movement of sediments from one location to another. Rivers, wind, ice, waves, groundwater
Deposition Accumulation of transported material when transporting energy decreases. All major geomorphic agents

Weathering Types

Physical weathering: mechanical breakdown without major chemical change.

Chemical weathering: alteration of minerals through chemical reactions.

Biological weathering: breakdown or alteration caused by organisms and their activities.

Mnemonic: P-C-B → Physical → Chemical → Biological

Coriolis Force and Geomorphic/Atmospheric Processes

The Coriolis effect is the apparent deflection of moving objects when viewed in a rotating reference frame such as Earth.

Earth's rotation + moving air/water → apparent deflection of path → Coriolis effect
  • Northern Hemisphere: deflection to the right of the direction of motion.
  • Southern Hemisphere: deflection to the left.
  • Deflection increases with latitude and generally with the speed of the moving object.
  • It is effectively zero at the Equator.
Important correction: The Coriolis force does not create the pressure gradient that drives wind. The pressure-gradient force initiates atmospheric motion; Coriolis deflects the moving air because Earth rotates.

Coriolis effects are important in:

  • Atmospheric circulation
  • Ocean currents
  • Cyclone rotation
  • Large-scale movement of water and air

Why Geomorphic Processes Matter

Endogenic forces → construct / deform relief
Exogenic forces → weather / erode / transport / deposit
Endogenic uplift → relief formation → exogenic erosion → sediment transport/deposition → landscape adjustment

Earth's landscapes therefore result from the continuous interaction between tectonic construction and surface denudation.

Mains framework: Instead of explaining a landform through a single process, examine the tectonic setting + rock type + climate + geomorphic agent + time.

Common Prelims Traps

Trap Correct Concept
Fold mountains are formed by faulting. Folding is dominant; faulting and thrusting may accompany mountain building.
Block mountains are formed by compression only. Usually associated with faulting under extensional/tensional conditions, though fault systems can arise under different stress regimes.
Normal fault = hanging wall rises. Normal fault → hanging wall moves down.
Reverse fault = hanging wall moves down. Reverse fault → hanging wall moves up.
Thrust fault is a separate force from reverse fault. A thrust fault is a low-angle reverse fault.
Horst = down-dropped block. Horst = uplifted block; graben = down-dropped block.
Rift valley is formed mainly by compression. Rift valley → crustal extension + normal faulting.
Africa has already split into two continents. East African rifting is an ongoing process; a future ocean basin may eventually develop.
All mountains are fold mountains. Mountains can be fold, block, dome, volcanic and residual/relic.
Volcanic mountains are always formed at convergent boundaries. They can form at subduction zones, divergent settings and hotspots.
Residual mountains are newly uplifted young mountains. They are commonly erosional remnants of older landscapes.
Anticline always has young rocks at its core. In a normal, non-overturned sequence, anticline core generally contains older rocks.
Syncline always has older rocks at its core. In a normal sequence, syncline core generally contains younger rocks.
All folds have equal limbs. Folds can be symmetrical, asymmetrical, isoclinal, overturned, recumbent and more.
Coriolis force drives winds. Pressure-gradient force initiates motion; Coriolis deflects moving air.
Coriolis is strongest at the Equator. Zero at Equator; increases towards the poles.
Exogenic processes only destroy landforms. They also transport and deposit sediments, creating depositional landforms.
