## Universe: Basic Framework
-------------------------------------------------------

Physical Geography studies natural processes and features of the Earth; understanding the universe provides the larger astronomical context for the Solar System and Earth.

The universe consists of matter, energy, space and spacetime and includes galaxies, stars, planets, gas, dust, black holes and other cosmic structures.

-------------------------------------------------------
## Origin and Evolution of the Universe
-------------------------------------------------------

[TABLE]
Theory | Core idea | UPSC relevance
Big Bang Theory | The universe evolved from an extremely hot, dense early state and has expanded and cooled over time. | Leading cosmological model; understand evidence and sequence.
Steady-State Theory | The universe was considered eternal; continuous creation of matter was proposed to maintain a roughly constant average density during expansion. | Historical theory; contrast with Big Bang.
Pulsating/Oscillating Theory | Universe alternates between expansion and contraction. | Historical/alternative model; not the accepted expectation for the observed accelerating universe.
[END TABLE]

### Steady-State Theory

Associated with Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle.

[FLOW]
Expansion of universe → dilution of matter → continuous creation of matter → average density remains approximately constant
[/FLOW]

It rejected a unique beginning of the universe. The discovery of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) strongly undermined the original steady-state picture.

### Pulsating/Oscillating Theory

[FLOW]
Expansion → maximum expansion → contraction → highly compressed state → renewed expansion
[/FLOW]

The present universe is known to be undergoing accelerated expansion, so a future universal contraction is not the mainstream expectation.

### Big Bang Theory

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old and evolved from an extremely hot and dense early state.

[FLOW]

Hot, dense early universe → rapid expansion and cooling → subatomic particles → protons and neutrons → Big Bang nucleosynthesis → hydrogen, helium and trace lithium → neutral atoms form about 380,000 years later → CMB photons travel freely → density fluctuations grow under gravity → stars → galaxies → present universe

[/FLOW]

[CONCEPT CLARIFICATION / CORRECTION]

Correction: Atomic nuclei, not complete neutral atoms, formed during the first few minutes. Neutral atoms formed much later, roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang.

[/CLARIFICATION]

### Big Bang nucleosynthesis

During the first few minutes, protons and neutrons combined to form light nuclei, mainly hydrogen and helium, with tiny amounts of lithium.

[EXAM FOCUS]

[PRELIMS] 
Do not confuse Big Bang nucleosynthesis with stellar nucleosynthesis. The former mainly explains the primordial light elements; stars create progressively heavier elements, while elements beyond the iron group require different high-energy/nucleosynthetic processes.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Singularity

A cosmological or gravitational singularity is a limit where classical General Relativity predicts extreme curvature/density and ceases to provide a reliable physical description.

## Evidence for the Big Bang
-------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Galactic Redshift

[FLOW]
Distant galaxy → light travels through expanding space → wavelength is stretched → spectral lines shift toward red → evidence of cosmic expansion
[/FLOW]

Redshift means an increase in observed wavelength and a decrease in frequency.

In cosmology, the observed relation is commonly expressed through the Hubble–Lemaître law: greater distance generally corresponds to greater recession velocity.

[EXAM FOCUS]

[PRELIMS] 
Cosmological redshift is primarily a consequence of the expansion of space. It should not be reduced to the simple idea that galaxies are merely moving through an otherwise static space.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### 2. Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMB)

[FLOW]
Hot early universe → expansion and cooling → neutral atoms form → photons decouple from matter → photons travel freely → cosmic expansion stretches wavelengths → microwave background observed today
[/FLOW]

* Temperature: approximately 2.725 K

* Region: microwave

* Represents the afterglow of the hot early universe

* First detected by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson in 1964

* Tiny fluctuations in CMB temperature trace early density variations that later contributed to large-scale cosmic structure.

### 3. Primordial abundance of light elements

The observed abundance of hydrogen and helium broadly agrees with predictions from Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

### 4. Large-scale structure

The distribution and evolution of galaxies and galaxy clusters are consistent with a universe that evolved from early density fluctuations under the action of gravity.

[EXAM FOCUS]

[PRELIMS] 
A classic UPSC-type distinction: redshift + CMB are key observational evidence associated with cosmic expansion and a hot early universe.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## General Relativity, Gravitational Waves and Lensing
-------------------------------------------------------

### General Theory of Relativity

Einstein's General Relativity, published in 1915, describes gravity as a consequence of the geometry of spacetime.

[FLOW]
Mass-energy → curvature of spacetime → motion follows curved spacetime → gravitational effects
[/FLOW]

### Gravitational Waves

Gravitational waves are propagating disturbances in spacetime generated by accelerating massive systems with changing quadrupole structure.

Important sources include binary black-hole mergers, binary neutron-star systems and suitable asymmetric stellar explosions.

* They travel through vacuum at the speed of light.

* They carry information about violent cosmic events.

* They produce extremely small stretching and compression of spacetime.

* Einstein predicted them in 1916.

### LIGO: principle and detection

[SCIENTIFIC PRINCIPLE]

LIGO is based on laser interferometry, specifically a Michelson-type interferometer.

Laser → beam splitter → two perpendicular arms → mirrors → reflected beams return → beams recombine → interference pattern

[/SCIENTIFIC PRINCIPLE]

In normal operation the detector is arranged so that the returning beams produce nearly complete destructive interference at the photodetector.

[FLOW]
Gravitational wave passes → one arm is stretched while the perpendicular arm is compressed → differential arm length changes → phase difference develops → destructive interference changes → detector records the change
[/FLOW]

[CONCEPT CLARIFICATION / CORRECTION]

Correction: LIGO is not based on “divergent interference”. The relevant principle is interference of coherent laser light in a Michelson-type interferometer; the baseline is arranged for destructive interference, and gravitational waves alter that interference.

[/CLARIFICATION]

### First direct detection and LIGO-India

2015: GW150914 — LIGO directly detected gravitational waves from the merger of two black holes.

LIGO-India is planned at Aundha, Hingoli, Maharashtra, as an international gravitational-wave observatory forming part of the global detector network.

### Why gravitational waves matter

* Open a new observational window beyond ordinary electromagnetic astronomy.

* Help study black holes, neutron stars, mergers and strong-gravity environments.

* Provide information about objects that may be difficult to observe through light.

* Improve understanding of the dynamics and evolution of the universe.

### Gravitational Lensing

[FLOW]
Massive foreground object → curves spacetime → light from background object bends → apparent position/shape/brightness changes
[/FLOW]

Einstein's General Relativity explains gravitational lensing. A sufficiently massive galaxy, galaxy cluster or compact object can act as a gravitational lens.

[TABLE]
Type | Main feature | Use
Strong lensing | Multiple images, arcs or Einstein rings | Study distant sources and foreground mass distribution
Weak lensing | Small statistical distortions in many background galaxies | Map dark matter and large-scale mass distribution
Microlensing | Temporary magnification when a foreground object passes near the line of sight | Detect otherwise faint/invisible objects; useful for some exoplanet searches
[END TABLE]

Einstein Ring: observer, lens and distant source are nearly perfectly aligned, producing a ring-like image.

## Black Holes
-------------------------------------------------------

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that, beyond the event horizon, no signal can escape to a distant observer.

[TABLE]
Feature | Meaning
Event horizon | Boundary beyond which escape to the outside is impossible.
Singularity | Central theoretical region where classical General Relativity predicts extreme curvature/density.
Accretion disk | Hot, rotating gas and matter around a black hole; can produce intense radiation.
Photon sphere | Region near a black hole where photons can follow unstable circular orbits.
Ergosphere | Region outside the event horizon of a rotating black hole where frame dragging is important.
[END TABLE]

### Formation

[FLOW]
Massive star → fuel exhausted → iron-group core develops → no further energy-producing core fusion → core collapses → supernova/core-collapse event → neutron star or black hole
[/FLOW]

Black holes can also form through direct gravitational collapse in sufficiently massive systems.

### Iron in the stellar core: the key nuclear-fusion link

[FLOW]
Hydrogen burning → helium burning → carbon/oxygen burning → neon/oxygen burning → silicon burning → iron-group core
[/FLOW]

For massive stars, fusion progressively builds heavier nuclei. Once an iron-group core dominates, further ordinary fusion cannot provide net energy to support the star because fusion beyond iron does not release usable energy.

[FLOW]
Iron-group core → energy-producing fusion stops → pressure support weakens → gravity wins → rapid core collapse → supernova → neutron star / black hole
[/FLOW]

[EXAM FOCUS]

[MAINS] 
The iron-core stage creates a direct link between stellar nucleosynthesis, energy balance, core collapse, supernovae and compact remnants.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Black-hole examples

* Cygnus X-1: famous X-ray binary containing a stellar-mass black hole candidate/confirmed black hole.

* Sagittarius A*: supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way.

* M87*: supermassive black hole whose surrounding emission/shadow was imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019.

## Standard Model of Particle Physics
-------------------------------------------------------

The Standard Model describes known elementary particles and three fundamental interactions:

[SEQUENCE: 
[Electromagnetic force] + 
[Strong nuclear force] + 
[Weak nuclear force]
]

Gravity is not included in the Standard Model.

[TABLE]
Particle | Key role
Quarks | Six types: up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom. They form composite particles such as protons and neutrons.
Leptons | Electron, muon, tau and their corresponding neutrinos.
Photon | Carrier of electromagnetic interaction.
Gluons | Carry the strong interaction.
W and Z bosons | Mediate the weak interaction.
Higgs boson | Quantum excitation associated with the Higgs field and the mechanism responsible for elementary particle masses.
[END TABLE]

[NOTE]

Higgs trap: It is inaccurate to say simply that the Higgs boson gives mass to everything. The Higgs mechanism gives mass to elementary particles that interact with the Higgs field; much of the mass of protons and neutrons arises from strong-interaction dynamics.

[/NOTE]

Limitations: the Standard Model does not adequately explain gravity, dark matter and dark energy, and leaves major questions in particle physics unresolved.

## Star Formation and Stellar Evolution
-------------------------------------------------------

### Nebular Hypothesis

[FLOW]
Giant gas and dust cloud → gravitational collapse → increasing rotation → conservation of angular momentum → flattened protoplanetary disk → central protostar/Sun → planets form from remaining disk material
[/FLOW]

Kant proposed an early nebular hypothesis; Laplace later revised the idea. Modern planetary science uses a much more developed solar nebular/protoplanetary disk model.

### Life cycle of a star

Dense regions within molecular clouds collapse under gravity and form protostars. Once the core becomes hot and dense enough for sustained hydrogen fusion, a star enters the main sequence.

### Low / intermediate mass

[FLOW]
Nebula → protostar → main sequence → red giant → outer layers expelled → planetary nebula → white dwarf
[/FLOW]

### Massive star

[FLOW]
Nebula → protostar → main sequence → red supergiant → successive fusion → iron-group core → core collapse → supernova → neutron star / black hole
[/FLOW]

### Main Sequence

The main sequence is the longest and most stable phase for most stars.

[FLOW]
Hydrogen fusion in core → helium formation + energy release → outward pressure balances gravitational contraction → stable star
[/FLOW]

### Red Giant and Red Supergiant

Red Giant: in a low/intermediate-mass star, core hydrogen is exhausted, the core contracts, shell hydrogen burning intensifies, and the outer layers expand and cool.

Red Supergiant: massive star in a late evolutionary stage with enormous size, cooler surface and extensive fusion in successive shells.

### Supernova, neutron star and white dwarf

[FLOW]
Massive star → iron-group core → core collapse → supernova → neutron star or black hole
[/FLOW]

Supernovae distribute newly synthesized material into interstellar space and help enrich later generations of stars and planets.

Neutron star: extremely dense compact remnant, typically only a few tens of kilometres across, dominated by neutron-rich matter and capable of very rapid rotation and intense magnetic fields.

White dwarf: compact remnant of a low/intermediate-mass star, supported mainly by electron degeneracy pressure; it cools gradually because ordinary nuclear fusion has ceased.

### Chandrasekhar Limit

Approximately 1.4 times the mass of the Sun is the characteristic maximum mass for a stable electron-degenerate white dwarf under standard assumptions.

### Red Dwarfs

Low-mass, cool, reddish main-sequence stars. They are extremely common and have very long lifetimes because they consume their nuclear fuel relatively slowly and efficiently.

### White dwarf → black dwarf

A black dwarf is a theoretical, fully cooled white dwarf. The universe is not old enough for ordinary white dwarfs to have reached this stage.

## Constellations
-------------------------------------------------------

A constellation is a recognised pattern/region of the sky. The stars appearing in a constellation are not necessarily physically close to one another.

The International Astronomical Union recognises 88 constellations.

### Important examples

[TABLE]
Constellation | Key facts
Orion | Prominent winter constellation in the Northern Hemisphere. Important stars include Betelgeuse and Rigel; Orion's Belt contains three prominent stars.
Ursa Major | Great Bear / Big Dipper / Saptarishi. The line through Dubhe and Merak points approximately toward Polaris.
Ursa Minor | Little Bear / Little Dipper; contains Polaris, close to the direction of the North Celestial Pole.
[END TABLE]

Celestial navigation: known stars and their apparent positions can be used to determine direction and, with suitable observations, location.

## Galaxies and the Milky Way
-------------------------------------------------------

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system containing stars, gas, dust, stellar remnants and dark matter.

[TABLE]
Type | Characteristics | Examples / note
Spiral | Disk, central bulge and spiral arms; generally contains substantial gas and dust and ongoing star formation. | Milky Way; Andromeda.
Barred Spiral | Central bar-shaped stellar structure with spiral arms extending from the bar. | Milky Way.
Elliptical | Rounded/ellipsoidal appearance; generally little cold gas and dust; mostly older stars and low current star formation. | Common in galaxy clusters.
Irregular | No well-defined regular structure; interactions can strongly disturb shape. | Often gas-rich and capable of active star formation.
[END TABLE]

### Milky Way Galaxy

[FLOW]
Milky Way = barred spiral galaxy → central bulge + disk/arms + halo → Sagittarius A* at the centre → Solar System located in the disk in the Orion Spur
[/FLOW]

* Diameter: approximately 100,000 light-years order of magnitude.

* Solar System distance from Galactic Centre: roughly 26,000–27,000 light-years.

* Solar orbital period around the Galactic Centre: about 225–250 million years.

* Central black hole: Sagittarius A*.

### Galaxy vs constellation

[TABLE]
Galaxy | Constellation
Physical gravitationally bound system. | Apparent pattern/defined region in the sky.
Contains enormous numbers of stars and other matter. | Contains a set of stars used to define a recognised sky pattern.
Stars are physically associated within the galactic system. | Stars can be at very different distances from Earth and from one another.
[END TABLE]
