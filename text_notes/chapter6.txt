## 1. Earth's Interior: Structure and Sources of Information
-------------------------------------------------------

Direct observation of Earth's deep interior is impossible because pressure and temperature increase enormously with depth. Hence, scientists reconstruct Earth's interior using both direct and indirect sources of evidence.

[TABLE]
Source | What it tells us
Deep mining and drilling | Direct information about rocks and temperatures at relatively shallow depths.
Volcanic material | Lava, volcanic rocks and xenoliths provide information about materials from depth.
Seismic waves | Changes in velocity, direction and paths reveal the internal structure and physical state of Earth's layers.
Meteorites | Provide clues about the probable composition of planetary building material and Earth's interior.
Gravity | Gravity anomalies reveal variations in density and mass distribution beneath the surface.
Geomagnetism | Provides information about Earth's magnetic materials and the electrically conducting outer core.
Geothermal observations | Temperature increases with depth and provides evidence about Earth's internal heat.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Prelims trap: Seismic waves, gravity, geomagnetism and meteorites are indirect sources. Deep mining/drilling and volcanic material provide comparatively direct evidence. Seismic waves are the most important tool for reconstructing the deep interior.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## 2. Chemical and Mechanical Structure of the Earth
-------------------------------------------------------

### Crust

The crust is Earth's outermost solid layer.

[TABLE]
Feature | Continental Crust | Oceanic Crust
Composition | Relatively silica-rich and alumina-rich; broadly granitic/felsic. | Relatively rich in iron and magnesium; broadly basaltic/mafic.
Thickness | Generally thicker; average ~35 km, but can exceed 70 km beneath major mountain systems. | Generally thinner; about 5–10 km.
Density | Lower, roughly 2.7 g/cm³. | Higher, roughly 2.9–3.0 g/cm³.
Age | Contains very old rocks. | Generally much younger because old oceanic crust is recycled by subduction.
Important process | Continental blocks are relatively buoyant. | Oceanic crust is commonly recycled into the mantle at subduction zones.
[END TABLE]

[CONCEPT CLARIFICATION / CORRECTION]

Terminology correction: SIAL (silica + alumina) and SIMA (silica + magnesium) are traditional descriptive terms. Modern geology primarily uses compositional terms such as felsic/granitic continental crust and mafic/basaltic oceanic crust. SIAL/SIMA are useful for UPSC only as historical shorthand.

[/CLARIFICATION]

### Mechanical layers

[FLOW]

Lithosphere = crust + uppermost rigid mantle

[/FLOW]

Lithosphere is rigid and is broken into tectonic plates.

Asthenosphere lies beneath the lithosphere and is mechanically weaker and capable of slow flow over geological time. It is not simply a completely molten layer; it is predominantly solid but may contain a small melt fraction depending on conditions.

[FLOW]

Lithosphere → rigid tectonic plates → move over the mechanically weaker asthenosphere

[/FLOW]

[TABLE]
Lithosphere | Asthenosphere
Rigid | Mechanically weak / ductile
Includes crust + uppermost mantle | Lies below lithosphere within upper mantle
Broken into tectonic plates | Allows plates to move over geological time
[END TABLE]

## 3. Mantle and Core
-------------------------------------------------------

### Mantle

The mantle extends from the Moho to the core–mantle boundary at about 2,900 km depth.

* Constitutes about 83% of Earth's volume.

* Composed predominantly of silicate minerals rich in magnesium and iron.

* Important minerals include olivine and pyroxene.

* The mantle is mostly solid but can deform and flow very slowly over geological time.

[FLOW]

Upper mantle → transition zones → lower mantle → core–mantle boundary

[/FLOW]

The mantle is important for convection, mantle plumes and transfer of heat, which are fundamental to plate tectonics and volcanism.

[NOTE]

Important distinction: Mantle is not a giant ocean of liquid magma. Most of it is solid rock capable of slow ductile flow.

[/NOTE]

### Core

The core begins at about 2,900 km and extends to Earth's centre at about 6,371 km.

It is composed predominantly of iron and nickel, along with lighter elements.

[FLOW]

Core → Outer Core + Inner Core

[/FLOW]

[TABLE]
Feature | Outer Core | Inner Core
Depth | ~2,900–5,150 km | ~5,150–6,371 km
State | Liquid | Solid
Main composition | Iron + nickel + lighter elements | Iron + nickel, with some lighter elements
Seismic behaviour | S-waves cannot propagate through it | P- and S-waves can propagate through it
Importance | Convection of conducting fluid → geodynamo | Solid inner core; contributes to Earth's deep structure and interacts with the outer-core system
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Most important Prelims distinction:

Outer core = liquid → no S-waves

Inner core = solid → S-waves can pass

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Why is the inner core solid despite extreme temperature?

Very high pressure at Earth's centre raises the melting temperature of iron-rich material, allowing the inner core to remain solid even at extremely high temperatures.

## 4. Discontinuities Inside the Earth
-------------------------------------------------------

A discontinuity is a zone where seismic-wave velocity and/or other physical properties change sharply, indicating a change in composition or physical state.

[TABLE]
Discontinuity | Separates | Approx. depth
Conrad | Upper and lower continental crust | Variable; present mainly in continental crust
Mohorovičić (Moho) | Crust and mantle | ~5–10 km beneath oceans; ~30–70+ km beneath continents
Repetti | Upper and lower mantle | ~670 km, approximate
Gutenberg | Mantle and outer core | ~2,900 km
Lehmann | Outer core and inner core | ~5,150 km
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: CMRGL

Conrad → Moho → Repetti → Gutenberg → Lehmann

[/EXAM FOCUS]

[FLOW]

Surface → Conrad → Moho → Repetti → Gutenberg → Lehmann → Centre

[/FLOW]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Ultra-high-yield:

Moho = crust–mantle

Gutenberg = mantle–outer core

Lehmann = outer core–inner core

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## 5. Temperature, Pressure, Density and Geothermal Energy
-------------------------------------------------------

Temperature generally increases with depth, although the rate of increase changes significantly between Earth's layers.

The geothermal gradient is much stronger near the surface than in the deep mantle and core.

[FLOW]

Depth increases → pressure increases → temperature generally increases → density generally increases

[/FLOW]

### Sources of Earth's internal heat

* Primordial heat left from planetary formation and differentiation.

* Radioactive decay of isotopes such as uranium, thorium and potassium.

* Additional heat associated with the evolution and cooling/crystallisation of the core.

Earth's internal heat drives important geological processes, including mantle convection, volcanism and plate tectonic activity.

### Gravity and density variations

Gravity varies spatially because Earth's mass is not distributed uniformly.

* Gravity is generally slightly stronger near the poles than at the equator.

* Local gravity anomalies reveal differences in the density and distribution of rocks beneath Earth's surface.

## 6. Seismic Waves: The Key to Earth's Interior
-------------------------------------------------------

Seismic waves are waves of energy released during earthquakes and other sudden disturbances. Their behaviour reveals the internal structure of Earth.

They are divided into:

[FLOW]

Seismic waves → Body waves + Surface waves

[/FLOW]

### Body Waves

[TABLE]
Feature | P-Waves | S-Waves
Full name | Primary waves | Secondary waves
Type | Longitudinal / compressional | Transverse / shear
Particle motion | Parallel to direction of propagation | Perpendicular to direction of propagation
Speed | Faster | Slower
Medium | Solids, liquids and gases | Solids only
Important use | Pass through core but change speed/direction substantially | Cannot pass through liquid outer core
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: P = Push + Passes liquids

S = Shake + Stops in liquid

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Surface Waves

Surface waves travel mainly along Earth's surface and are responsible for much of the destructive ground motion during earthquakes.

[TABLE]
Wave | Movement
Love waves | Horizontal, side-to-side shear motion; no significant vertical displacement.
Rayleigh waves | Rolling, elliptical particle motion with both horizontal and vertical components.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic:

Love = Lateral

Rayleigh = Rolling

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Order of seismic-wave speed

[FLOW]

P-waves → S-waves → surface waves

[/FLOW]

Surface waves are generally the slowest and usually arrive last at a seismograph.

### Why P-waves are faster than S-waves

P-waves propagate by compression and expansion, while S-waves require shear deformation of the medium. Solids resist compression and shear differently, so the two wave types travel at different speeds.

-------------------------------------------------------
## 7. Seismic Shadow Zones
-------------------------------------------------------

Changes in seismic-wave velocity and direction as waves enter different layers provide evidence about Earth's internal structure.

[FLOW]

P-wave enters liquid outer core → velocity decreases + wave refracts → large region with no direct P-waves develops

[/FLOW]

[FLOW]

S-wave reaches liquid outer core → cannot propagate through liquid → S-wave shadow zone

[/FLOW]

The major P-wave shadow zone lies approximately between 105° and 140°–145° from the earthquake epicentre, while direct S-waves are not detected beyond roughly 105°.

[EXAM FOCUS]

UPSC favourite:

S-wave disappearance → liquid outer core

P-wave refraction at core → P-wave shadow zone

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### How the core was inferred

[FLOW]

Earthquake → P and S waves generated → waves travel through Earth → sudden changes in velocity/path → identify boundaries → infer solid/liquid layers

[/FLOW]

## 8. Earthquakes: Origin, Focus and Epicentre
-------------------------------------------------------

An earthquake is the sudden release of accumulated elastic energy in Earth's crust/lithosphere, producing seismic waves and ground motion.

### Elastic Rebound Theory

[FLOW]

Tectonic stress accumulates along a fault → rocks deform elastically → friction keeps fault locked → stress exceeds rock strength → sudden slip → stored elastic energy released → seismic waves → earthquake

[/FLOW]

The theory was developed from observations following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and is fundamental to understanding fault earthquakes.

### Focus and Epicentre

Focus / Hypocentre: point inside Earth where rupture begins and seismic energy is first released.

Epicentre: point on Earth's surface directly above the focus.

[FLOW]

Focus underground → vertical projection to surface → Epicentre

[/FLOW]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: Focus = First underground; Epicentre = Earth-surface point

[/EXAM FOCUS]

-------------------------------------------------------
## 9. Causes of Earthquakes
-------------------------------------------------------

### Natural causes

[TABLE]
Cause | Mechanism
Faulting / tectonic movement | Stress builds up because plates move, lock and suddenly slip.
Subduction | One plate sinks beneath another, producing frequent and sometimes very deep earthquakes.
Volcanism | Magma movement, pressure changes and volcanic activity can generate earthquakes.
[END TABLE]

[FLOW]

Plate movement → stress accumulation → friction/locking → fault rupture → earthquake

[/FLOW]

### Anthropogenic / induced earthquakes

* Reservoir-induced seismicity: water loading and pore-pressure changes associated with large reservoirs can trigger earthquakes.

* Mining: removal of rock alters stress distribution.

* Oil and gas extraction: pressure changes can destabilise faults.

* Wastewater injection: increases pore pressure and can reactivate faults.

* Hydraulic fracturing: injection can induce small to moderate seismic events in some settings.

* Geothermal operations: fluid injection/extraction can trigger seismicity.

* Underground construction and some other subsurface activities can modify local stresses.

-------------------------------------------------------
## 10. Earthquake Swarm, Foreshock, Mainshock and Aftershock
-------------------------------------------------------

Earthquake swarm: sequence of numerous mostly small earthquakes concentrated in a particular area over a relatively short period, without a single clearly dominant mainshock.

Foreshock: earthquake occurring before a larger event that is subsequently identified as the mainshock.

Mainshock: largest principal earthquake in a sequence.

Aftershocks: earthquakes following the mainshock as the crust adjusts to the changed stress conditions.

[FLOW]

Foreshock(s) → Mainshock → Aftershocks

[/FLOW]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Trap: An earthquake is called a foreshock only after a larger subsequent earthquake occurs. Before that, it cannot be known with certainty that it is a foreshock.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

-------------------------------------------------------
## 11. Distribution of Earthquakes
-------------------------------------------------------

Earthquakes are concentrated mainly along tectonic plate boundaries.

### Circum-Pacific Belt / Ring of Fire

[FLOW]

Pacific plate boundaries + multiple subduction zones → intense tectonic activity → major concentration of earthquakes + volcanoes → Circum-Pacific Belt / Ring of Fire

[/FLOW]

The Pacific region contains the world's greatest concentration of active volcanoes and frequent earthquakes.

### Other major seismic belts

* Alpine-Himalayan Belt: extends from the Mediterranean through southern Europe and Asia towards the Himalayas.

* Mid-ocean ridges: earthquakes associated mainly with divergent plate boundaries.

* East African Rift region: earthquakes associated with continental rifting.

* Indian region: Himalaya, northeast India, Andaman–Nicobar and parts of peninsular India show varying seismic activity.

### Himalayan earthquakes

The Himalaya is highly seismic because the Indian Plate continues to converge with the Eurasian Plate.

[FLOW]

Indian Plate → northward convergence → compression + faulting → stress accumulation → Himalayan earthquakes

[/FLOW]

### Wadati–Benioff Zone

A Wadati–Benioff zone is an inclined zone of earthquakes extending downward from a subduction zone into the mantle.

[FLOW]

Oceanic plate subducts → slab descends → earthquake foci follow descending slab → Wadati–Benioff zone

[/FLOW]

It provides strong evidence for subduction and plate tectonics.

## 12. Measuring Earthquakes
-------------------------------------------------------

### Magnitude vs Intensity

[TABLE]
Magnitude | Intensity
Measures the size/energy released at the source. | Measures the observed effects and shaking at a location.
Essentially one value for a given earthquake using a specified magnitude scale. | Can vary from place to place.
Modern earthquake science commonly uses Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw). | Commonly described using the Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: Magnitude = Machine/source; Intensity = Impact/place

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Richter Scale

The Richter scale was developed by Charles F. Richter for measuring earthquake magnitude using seismic-wave amplitudes.

It is historically important, but for large modern earthquakes the preferred scale is the Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw).

### Seismograph

A seismograph/seismometer detects and records ground motion produced by seismic waves.

[FLOW]

Earthquake → ground motion → seismometer → recorded seismic signal → analysis of arrival times/amplitudes → earthquake location + magnitude + interior information

[/FLOW]

## 13. Earthquake Hazards and Secondary Effects
-------------------------------------------------------

Earthquake risk is not determined by magnitude alone. It depends on hazard + exposure + vulnerability.

[TABLE]
Hazard | Effect
Ground shaking | Structural damage and collapse.
Surface rupture | Ground displacement along a fault.
Liquefaction | Water-saturated loose sediments temporarily lose strength during strong shaking.
Landslides | Shaking destabilises slopes, especially in mountainous terrain.
Tsunami | Undersea earthquake, especially involving sudden vertical seafloor displacement, can generate destructive sea waves.
Fires | Damaged gas/electricity infrastructure can cause fires.
Dam failure | Strong shaking can damage dams and trigger downstream flooding.
Displacement | Damage to homes and infrastructure can displace populations.
[END TABLE]

[FLOW]

Earthquake → ground shaking → infrastructure damage → secondary hazards → human + economic + environmental losses

[/FLOW]

## 14. Earthquake Risk in India
-------------------------------------------------------

India is highly earthquake-prone because of its tectonic setting, especially the continuing collision of the Indian Plate with Eurasia.

Major vulnerable regions include:

* Himalayan region

* Northeast India

* Andaman and Nicobar Islands

* Parts of Gujarat, including the Kachchh region

* Other areas containing active or potentially active faults

### Indian seismic zoning

India's seismic zoning currently uses four zones: II, III, IV and V, with Zone V representing the highest seismic hazard.

[NOTE]

Important: Older books may show five zones, including Zone I. The present BIS framework uses four seismic zones.

[/NOTE]

[FLOW]

Zone II → Zone III → Zone IV → Zone V

[/FLOW]

Zone V: highest seismic hazard; includes much of the northeast, parts of the Himalaya, parts of Gujarat and the Andaman–Nicobar region.

-------------------------------------------------------
## 15. Earthquake Management in India
-------------------------------------------------------

[FLOW]

Hazard assessment → seismic zoning → earthquake-resistant building codes → retrofitting → early warning/monitoring → public awareness → emergency response → resilient reconstruction

[/FLOW]

* Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS): develops earthquake-resistant building standards.

* National Centre for Seismology (NCS): monitors seismic activity and provides earthquake information.

* NDMA: develops disaster-management guidelines and promotes preparedness and mitigation.

* SDMAs / local authorities: implement preparedness, response and risk-reduction measures.

* Microzonation: identifies local variations in seismic hazard within cities/regions.

* Retrofitting: strengthens existing vulnerable structures.

* Mock drills and public awareness: improve preparedness and response capacity.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mains framework: Prevention → Mitigation → Preparedness → Response → Recovery

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## 18. Common Prelims Traps
-------------------------------------------------------

[TABLE]
Trap | Correct concept
Outer core is solid because pressure is high. | Outer core is liquid. High pressure helps keep the inner core solid despite high temperature.
S-waves pass through liquids. | S-waves cannot pass through liquids.
P-waves cannot pass through liquids. | P-waves can pass through solids, liquids and gases.
Moho separates mantle and core. | Moho = crust–mantle boundary.
Gutenberg separates crust and mantle. | Gutenberg = mantle–outer core.
Lehmann separates mantle and core. | Lehmann = outer core–inner core.
Asthenosphere is a fully molten layer. | It is mainly solid but mechanically weak and capable of ductile flow.
Continental crust is denser than oceanic crust. | Oceanic crust is denser.
Oceanic crust is older than continental crust. | Most oceanic crust is relatively young because of continuous recycling at subduction zones.
Magnitude and intensity are identical. | Magnitude = source size/energy; intensity = local effects.
Richter is the universal modern scale for all earthquakes. | Moment Magnitude (Mw) is preferred for large modern earthquakes.
Earthquake swarm = foreshock sequence. | Swarm is a cluster of earthquakes without one obvious dominant mainshock; foreshocks are identified only after a larger event.
Every earthquake produces a tsunami. | Only some undersea earthquakes, especially those causing significant vertical seafloor displacement, generate tsunamis.
All earthquakes are tectonic. | Most are tectonic, but some are associated with volcanism or human-induced activities.
Ring of Fire is only a volcanic belt. | It is both a major seismic and volcanic belt, strongly associated with subduction.
Wadati–Benioff zone occurs at divergent boundaries. | It is primarily associated with subduction at convergent boundaries.
India has five current seismic zones. | The current BIS classification uses four zones: II, III, IV and V.
[END TABLE]
