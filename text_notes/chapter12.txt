## Exogenetic Processes: The Basic Framework
-------------------------------------------------------

Exogenetic processes are external geomorphic processes that operate at or near Earth's surface and progressively modify relief through weathering, mass wasting, erosion, transportation and deposition.

They are powered mainly by solar energy, gravity and the hydrological cycle, and operate in interaction with the relief created by endogenic forces.

[FLOW]

Endogenic uplift → relief/elevation increases → weathering + mass wasting + erosion → transport → deposition → relief is lowered/redistributed

[/FLOW]

This continuous lowering and leveling of relief is broadly described as denudation.

[CONCEPT CLARIFICATION / CORRECTION]

Important correction: Exogenetic processes do not simply “originate from the atmosphere”. They are driven mainly by solar energy, gravity, water, temperature differences and atmospheric processes, acting on Earth's surface.

[/CLARIFICATION]

[TABLE]
Process | Meaning | Main result
Weathering | Breakdown/decomposition of rock in situ. | Regolith and weathered material.
Mass wasting | Downslope movement of rock, soil or debris primarily under gravity. | Slope modification.
Erosion | Detachment and removal of material by a geomorphic agent. | Rock/sediment removal.
Transportation | Movement of eroded material by water, wind, ice, waves etc. | Redistribution of sediments.
Deposition | Accumulation of transported sediment when the transporting agent loses energy/carrying capacity. | New depositional landforms.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Core distinction: Weathering = breakdown | Erosion = removal | Transportation = movement | Deposition = accumulation | Mass wasting = downslope movement under gravity

[/EXAM FOCUS]

-------------------------------------------------------
## Interplay of Endogenic and Exogenic Processes
-------------------------------------------------------

Landforms are the product of a continuous competition between uplift and denudation.

[FLOW]

Tectonic uplift → mountains/highlands → enhanced slopes → greater erosion → sediment transport → deposition in lowlands/basins

[/FLOW]

Constructive geomorphology: processes that increase elevation or build relief through uplift, deposition or accumulation.

Destructive geomorphology: processes that wear down and remove existing relief through weathering, erosion and mass wasting.

Examples: Himalayan uplift provides high relief and steep slopes, which encourage intense erosion and landsliding. Sediments eroded from mountains are eventually deposited in foreland basins, floodplains and deltas.

## Weathering
-------------------------------------------------------

Weathering is the breakdown or decomposition of rocks and minerals at or near the Earth's surface without large-scale transport of the material.

[FLOW]

Rock exposed at surface → temperature/water/chemicals/organisms act on it → disintegration or decomposition → regolith → soil formation

[/FLOW]

Weathering is a major precursor to soil formation, erosion and mass wasting.

### Factors Controlling Weathering

* Climate: temperature, rainfall and freeze-thaw conditions.

* Rock type: mineral composition, hardness and permeability.

* Structure: joints, fractures, bedding planes and faults.

* Topography: slope and elevation influence exposure and drainage.

* Vegetation and organisms: roots and organic acids promote breakdown.

* Time: prolonged exposure increases weathering.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Climate rule: Hot + dry → mechanical weathering tends to dominate

Warm + humid → chemical weathering tends to dominate

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### 1. Mechanical / Physical Weathering

Mechanical weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces without substantially changing their chemical composition.

### A. Thermal Expansion and Contraction

[FLOW]

Strong heating during day → mineral expansion → cooling at night → contraction → repeated stress → cracks → rock disintegration

[/FLOW]

Important in desert and semi-arid regions where diurnal temperature ranges can be large.

Different minerals expand at different rates, producing stress at mineral boundaries.

### B. Freeze–Thaw / Frost Wedging

[FLOW]

Water enters crack → temperature falls below freezing → water expands as ice → crack widens → repeated freezing/thawing → rock fragments

[/FLOW]

Especially important in high mountains and high latitudes.

### C. Granular Disintegration

Different minerals expand and contract differently under temperature changes. Repeated stress at grain boundaries weakens the rock, eventually producing granular material.

### D. Exfoliation

Exfoliation is the peeling or flaking of outer rock layers, often producing curved sheets.

[FLOW]

Pressure release/unloading + thermal stress → expansion of outer layers → sheet joints → peeling → exfoliation

[/FLOW]

It is common in massive crystalline rocks such as granite.

### E. Unloading / Pressure Release

[FLOW]

Overlying material removed → pressure decreases → underlying rock expands → joints develop parallel to surface → sheets peel off

[/FLOW]

This is particularly important where deeply buried rocks become exposed through erosion.

### F. Other Physical Weathering

* Salt weathering: salt crystals grow in pores and cracks, producing pressure and granular disintegration.

* Wetting and drying: repeated expansion and contraction of clay-rich materials.

* Fire-induced weathering: rapid heating and cooling can fracture exposed rocks.

* Block disintegration: jointed rocks break into angular blocks.

### 2. Chemical Weathering

Chemical weathering changes the chemical composition and mineral structure of rocks.

[FLOW]

Water + oxygen + carbon dioxide + acids → chemical reactions with minerals → alteration/dissolution → new minerals + dissolved material

[/FLOW]

### A. Hydration

Hydration occurs when water molecules become incorporated into mineral structures.

It can increase mineral volume and weaken the rock.

### B. Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis involves reaction between water and minerals, especially silicate minerals.

[FLOW]

Water reacts with silicate minerals → minerals decompose → clay minerals + dissolved ions → weaker rock

[/FLOW]

Feldspar → clay minerals is an important example.

### C. Oxidation

Oxidation occurs when minerals react with oxygen.

[FLOW]

Iron-bearing mineral + O₂ → oxidation → iron oxides/hydroxides → weakening + reddish colour

[/FLOW]

Hence iron-rich rocks commonly develop red, yellow or brown weathering colours.

### D. Carbonation

[FLOW]

CO₂ dissolves in rainwater → carbonic acid forms → CaCO₃ dissolves → limestone weakens/dissolves → karst features

[/FLOW]

Carbonation is especially important in limestone regions.

### E. Solution

Some minerals dissolve directly in water. Soluble salts are especially susceptible.

### F. Chelation

Chelation involves organic compounds forming complexes with metal ions, increasing the mobility of those metals and weakening minerals.

It is promoted by plant roots, microorganisms and organic acids.

### G. Salt Weathering

[FLOW]

Saline water enters pores → evaporation → salt crystallisation → crystal pressure → cracking/disintegration

[/FLOW]

Common in arid, semi-arid and coastal environments.

### 3. Biological Weathering

Biological weathering results from the action of plants, animals and microorganisms.

* Root wedging: roots grow into joints and widen cracks.

* Burrowing organisms: animals disturb and loosen soil and rock material.

* Organic acids: plants, fungi and lichens release acids that dissolve minerals.

* Microorganisms: bacterial and fungal activity can chemically alter minerals.

[FLOW]

Biological activity → physical disturbance + organic acids → rock breakdown → enhanced weathering

[/FLOW]

### Weathering and Climate

[TABLE]
Environment | Dominant weathering tendency | Main reason
Hot + humid | Chemical | Abundant water + high temperature accelerate chemical reactions.
Hot + dry | Mechanical | Large temperature range + limited moisture.
Cold + wet | Freeze-thaw + chemical processes | Repeated freezing and thawing where temperatures fluctuate around freezing.
High mountains | Mechanical prominent | Freeze-thaw, pressure changes and rapid temperature fluctuations.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

UPSC 2024 Prelims: Rainfall can promote weathering because rainwater contains dissolved CO₂ and atmospheric oxygen, both of which contribute to chemical weathering.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Mass Wasting / Mass Movement
-------------------------------------------------------

Mass wasting is the downslope movement of weathered rock, soil or debris under the influence of gravity.

[FLOW]

Weathering + slope instability → material becomes loose/saturated → gravity acts → downslope movement → mass wasting

[/FLOW]

### Factors Increasing Mass Wasting

* Steep slopes

* Heavy rainfall / snowmelt

* Earthquakes and volcanic activity

* Weathering and weakening of rocks

* Removal of vegetation

* Road cutting and excavation

* Mining and quarrying

* Poor drainage and water saturation

* Glacial retreat/permafrost thaw in suitable environments

### Major Types of Mass Wasting

[TABLE]
Type | Movement | Main clue
Fall | Material detaches and falls freely. | Very rapid; cliffs/steep slopes.
Rockfall | Individual rocks/blocks fall from steep slopes. | Common in jointed mountain terrain.
Slide | Material moves along a relatively distinct failure surface. | Translational or rotational.
Slump | Rotational movement along a curved failure surface. | Backward rotation and spoon-shaped scar.
Flow | Material behaves more like a fluid. | Often associated with high water content.
Debris flow | Rapid mixture of water, mud, rock fragments and debris. | Fast and destructive.
Creep | Extremely slow downslope movement. | May produce tilted trees, poles and fences.
Solifluction | Slow downslope flow of water-saturated soil. | Common in cold/periglacial regions over frozen ground.
Debris avalanche | Very rapid movement of fragmented rock/debris. | Can occur after volcanic collapse or major slope failure.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: F-S-F-C → Falls + Slides + Flows + Creep

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Slump vs Flow

Slump: movement along a curved surface with rotational displacement.

Flow: material loses internal coherence and behaves more like a viscous fluid.

### Avalanches

An avalanche is a rapid downslope movement of snow, ice and sometimes rock/debris.

[FLOW]

Snow accumulation → weak layer/trigger → slope failure → snow accelerates downslope → avalanche

[/FLOW]

Triggers can include:

* Heavy snowfall

* Rapid warming

* Weak snow layers

* Earthquakes

* Wind loading

* Human disturbance

Gangotri Glacier avalanche events and Himalayan snow avalanches illustrate the hazard in India.

### Landslides

A landslide is a general term for downslope movement of rock, earth or debris. It can occur as a slide, fall, slump or flow.

[FLOW]

Heavy rain/earthquake/road cutting → slope strength decreases → shear stress exceeds resistance → slope failure → landslide

[/FLOW]

[TABLE]
Himalayas | Western Ghats
Young, tectonically active mountain system. | Older and comparatively stable mountain system.
Steep slopes + active uplift + frequent earthquakes. | Steep escarpments and deeply weathered/lateritic slopes in many areas.
Intense monsoon rainfall and cloudbursts. | Heavy monsoon rainfall.
Road construction, hydropower and urbanisation can destabilise slopes. | Quarrying, road cutting, agriculture and settlement expansion can increase instability.
[END TABLE]

UPSC framing: Landslides result from the interaction of susceptibility + trigger + human modification.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Case studies: Kedarnath region, Chamoli/Uttarakhand; Nagaland and Northeast India; Konkan–Western Ghats landslides during intense monsoon rainfall.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Erosion and Transportation
-------------------------------------------------------

Erosion involves the detachment and removal of rock/soil, while transportation moves the material away from its source.

[FLOW]

Weathering → loose material → erosion → transportation → deposition

[/FLOW]

Major erosional agents are:

[FLOW]

Running water + Groundwater + Glaciers + Wind + Waves/Currents

[/FLOW]

### Running Water

Running water is the most widespread erosional agent and produces extensive fluvial landforms.

### Sheet Erosion

A thin, relatively uniform layer of soil is removed by surface runoff.

[FLOW]

Rainfall → sheet runoff → uniform soil removal → soil depth decreases

[/FLOW]

### Rill Erosion

Runoff concentrates into small channels called rills.

### Gully Erosion

Rills enlarge into deeper, wider channels called gullies.

[FLOW]

Sheet erosion → rill erosion → gully erosion → severe soil loss / badland development

[/FLOW]

Indian example: Chambal ravines are strongly associated with gully erosion and badland topography.

### Corrosion / Solution

Chemical dissolution of soluble rock by water, especially important in limestone terrain.

### Hydraulic Action

Moving water exerts pressure on rock surfaces, especially on cracks, joints and weak zones, accelerating erosion.

### Abrasion / Corrasion

Sediment carried by water, ice or wind strikes and scrapes the underlying surface.

### Attrition

Transported particles collide with one another, becoming smaller, smoother and more rounded.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Remember: Abrasion = agent rubs the surface | Attrition = particles rub each other

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Fluvial Landforms
-------------------------------------------------------

[FLOW]

Upper course → youthful valley → waterfalls/gorges/V-shaped valleys → Middle course → meanders/floodplains → Lower course → deposition/deltas

[/FLOW]

### Upper Course

High gradient and strong vertical erosion dominate.

* V-shaped valleys

* Interlocking spurs

* Rapids

* Waterfalls

* Potholes

* Gorges

### V-shaped Valley

Strong vertical erosion cuts downward into the valley floor, producing a narrow valley with steep sides.

### Interlocking Spurs

Resistant projections of higher land jut alternately into the river valley, forcing the river to wind around them.

### Waterfalls

Waterfalls occur where there is a sudden drop in the river's long profile.

Causes include:

* Differential erosion of hard and soft rocks

* Faulting

* Tectonic uplift

* River rejuvenation

[FLOW]

Hard rock over soft rock → soft rock erodes faster → unsupported hard rock → plunge develops → waterfall retreats upstream → gorge

[/FLOW]

### Rapids

Rapid turbulent flow occurs where the river gradient changes abruptly, commonly due to resistant rocks or uneven bed structure.

### Potholes

Cylindrical or bowl-shaped depressions formed in riverbeds through abrasion by swirling water and sediment.

### Gorge and Canyon

[TABLE]
Gorge | Canyon
Narrow, steep-sided valley. | Typically broader/deeper valley with prominent steep walls.
Often associated with strong downcutting. | Can develop through prolonged erosion in arid/semi-arid settings.
Example: Indus gorge in the Himalaya. | Grand Canyon, USA.
[END TABLE]

### Meanders

A meander is a sinuous bend in a river channel.

[FLOW]

Faster flow on outer bend → lateral erosion → cut bank

[/FLOW]

[FLOW]

Slower flow on inner bend → deposition → point bar

[/FLOW]

Hence:

[FLOW]

Concave bank → erosion | Convex bank → deposition

[/FLOW]

Meanders are especially characteristic of the middle and lower courses.

### Interlocking to Meandering

[FLOW]

Upper course → steep gradient + vertical erosion → V-shaped valley → reduced gradient → lateral erosion increases → meanders develop

[/FLOW]

### Oxbow Lake

[FLOW]

Strongly curved meander → neck becomes narrow → erosion breaches neck → old loop cut off → oxbow lake

[/FLOW]

### Floodplain

A floodplain is a relatively flat valley floor built mainly by repeated deposition during floods.

It commonly contains:

* Natural levees

* Backswamps

* Meander scars

* Oxbow lakes

* Point bars

### Natural Levees

Raised embankments along river banks formed when floodwaters overflow and deposit relatively coarse sediment close to the channel.

[FLOW]

Flood → river overtops bank → velocity falls → coarse sediment deposited near channel → natural levee

[/FLOW]

### Braided Channels

Braided rivers have multiple interconnected channels separated by temporary or semi-permanent sediment bars.

They are favoured by:

* High sediment load

* Variable discharge

* Steep gradients

* Weak, erodible banks

### Alluvial Fans and Cones

An alluvial fan is a fan-shaped depositional body formed where a stream emerges from a steep mountain valley onto a broad, flatter plain.

[FLOW]

Mountain valley → sudden decrease in slope → stream loses energy → coarse sediment deposited near apex → finer sediment outward → alluvial fan

[/FLOW]

An alluvial cone is generally steeper and more cone-like than a broad alluvial fan.

Indian example: piedmont region at the foothills of the Shiwalik Himalayas.

### River Terraces

River terraces are step-like surfaces along valley sides representing remnants of former floodplains.

[FLOW]

Uplift / fall in base level / reduced sediment load → river rejuvenation → renewed downcutting → former floodplain abandoned as terrace

[/FLOW]

### Delta

A delta is a depositional landform formed near the mouth of a river where sediment accumulates faster than it is removed by waves, tides and currents.

### Conditions Favouring Delta Formation

* Large sediment supply

* Gentle offshore slope

* Relatively shallow coastal water

* Weak wave removal

* Weak tidal redistribution

* Stable coastline and sufficient accommodation space

[FLOW]

River enters sea → velocity falls sharply → sediment deposition → distributaries → delta growth

[/FLOW]

### Major Delta Types

[TABLE]
Type | Shape | Example
Arcuate / Lobate | Curved, fan-like or arc-shaped. | Nile; Ganges-Brahmaputra is strongly tide-influenced but has extensive deltaic development.
Bird-foot | Long distributaries project into the sea. | Mississippi.
Cuspate | Pointed seaward. | Tiber Delta.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

UPSC connection: Rivers draining the Western Ghats generally have short courses, steep gradients and limited sediment storage; many reach the sea before building large deltas, helping explain the contrast with major east-coast delta systems.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Estuary vs Delta

[TABLE]
Estuary | Delta
River mouth is strongly influenced by tidal action and marine processes. | Sediment accumulation dominates.
Usually funnel-shaped and relatively deep. | Commonly has distributaries and depositional plains.
Example: Narmada, Tapi. | Example: Ganges-Brahmaputra, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna.
[END TABLE]

## Groundwater Landforms: Karst Topography
-------------------------------------------------------

Karst topography develops mainly on soluble rocks such as limestone through groundwater dissolution.

[FLOW]

Rainwater + CO₂ → weak carbonic acid → limestone dissolution → enlarged joints/underground channels → caves + sinkholes + karst landscape

[/FLOW]

### Erosional Groundwater Landforms

[TABLE]
Landform | Description
Lapiés / Karren | Grooved, ridged limestone surface produced by solution.
Sinkhole / Doline | Closed depression produced by dissolution and/or collapse.
Solution hole | Hole enlarged through chemical dissolution.
Swallow hole | Opening through which surface drainage disappears underground.
Cave / Cavern | Underground void enlarged by dissolution.
Natural bridge | Rock bridge left when surrounding soluble rock is dissolved.
Karst valley | Valley system influenced strongly by solution and underground drainage.
[END TABLE]

### Depositional Groundwater Landforms

[FLOW]

Dissolution underground → groundwater becomes mineral-rich → pressure/temperature changes + CO₂ loss → CaCO₃ precipitates → cave deposits

[/FLOW]

[TABLE]
Feature | Where it forms
Stalactite | Hangs from cave roof.
Stalagmite | Grows upward from cave floor.
Column / Pillar | Forms when stalactite and stalagmite join.
Drapery / Curtain | Sheet-like cave deposit.
Flowstone | Sheet-like deposit formed by flowing/seeping water.
Helictite | Irregular growth projecting sideways or in unusual directions.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: Stalactite = ceiling | Stalagmite = ground

“Stalactite holds tight to the roof.”

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Wind as a Geomorphic Agent
-------------------------------------------------------

Wind is especially important in arid and semi-arid regions where vegetation is sparse and loose sediment is abundant.

[FLOW]

Strong winds → deflation + abrasion + transport → erosion → deposition where wind loses energy

[/FLOW]

### Wind Erosion Processes

[TABLE]
Process | Meaning
Deflation | Removal and lifting of loose fine particles.
Abrasion | Sand-laden wind mechanically wears down exposed rocks.
Attrition | Wind-blown grains collide with one another and become smaller/rounder.
[END TABLE]

### Erosional Landforms of Wind

* Deflation hollows / basins

* Mushroom rocks / pedestal rocks

* Inselbergs

* Yardangs

* Zeugen

* Wind bridges and windows

* Mesas and buttes in arid landscapes where differential erosion operates

### Mushroom Rock

Wind-driven sand is most effective near the ground, so the lower part of the rock is often eroded more strongly.

[FLOW]

Strong near-surface abrasion → lower rock erodes faster → narrow base + broader top → mushroom/pedestal rock

[/FLOW]

### Inselberg

An inselberg is an isolated residual hill or mountain rising abruptly above a relatively level surrounding landscape.

### Yardang

Yardangs are elongated streamlined ridges separated by grooves, produced by wind abrasion and deflation.

They are common in deserts where softer materials are removed faster than resistant layers.

### Zeugen

Zeugen develop through differential wind erosion where harder rock layers protect softer material beneath them.

### Wind Bridges and Windows

Wind abrasion can exploit joints and weak zones in rock, eventually producing openings and arches.

### Loess

Loess is a fine-grained, wind-blown deposit dominated largely by silt.

* Extensive loess deposits are found in China and parts of Central Asia and Europe.

* Loess soils can be highly fertile because of their mineral content.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Loess vs Dune: Loess = predominantly fine silt deposited by wind; dunes = mainly sand-sized particles shaped into mounds/ridges.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Sand Dunes
-------------------------------------------------------

Dunes are accumulations of sand formed by wind deposition.

[FLOW]

Wind transports sand → obstacle/roughness reduces wind speed → deposition → mound grows → dune migrates

[/FLOW]

Dunes may occur in deserts, coastal areas, riverine environments and near some lake shores.

### Major Types of Sand Dunes

[TABLE]
Type | Key characteristics
Barchan | Crescent-shaped; horns point downwind; develops where sand supply is limited and winds are relatively unidirectional.
Transverse | Long ridges oriented broadly perpendicular to prevailing wind; abundant sand supply.
Longitudinal / Seif | Long narrow ridges roughly parallel to prevailing wind; common under persistent wind regimes.
Parabolic | U-shaped; horns generally point upwind, often stabilised by vegetation.
Star | Multiple arms/ridges radiating from a central high point; develops where wind directions vary strongly.
Reversing | Develops where winds blow from two dominant opposite directions.
Dome | Rounded dune with limited slip-face development.
Nebkha | Sand accumulated around vegetation such as shrubs or grasses.
Whaleback | Long, elongated dune/ridge resembling the back of a whale.
[END TABLE]

[CONCEPT CLARIFICATION / CORRECTION]

Important correction: The source material reverses the horn orientation of parabolic dunes. In the standard classification, barchan horns point downwind, whereas parabolic dune horns point upwind, usually because vegetation stabilises the arms.

[/CLARIFICATION]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic:

Barchan → horns Blow Downwind

Parabolic → plants hold horns Upwind

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Coastal / Sea-Water Landforms
-------------------------------------------------------

Coastal landscapes are shaped by the interaction of waves, tides, currents, wind and sediment supply.

### Erosional Coastal Processes

* Hydraulic action: wave pressure on cracks and joints.

* Abrasion: rock fragments thrown against the coast by waves.

* Attrition: sediment particles collide and become smaller/rounder.

* Solution: chemical dissolution of susceptible rocks.

[FLOW]

Wave erosion → cracks enlarge → cave → arch → stack → stump

[/FLOW]

### Cliffs

Sea cliffs are steep, often near-vertical coastal slopes produced mainly by marine erosion and subaerial weathering.

### Wave-Cut Platform

[FLOW]

Wave erosion at cliff base → notch develops → cliff collapses → cliff retreats → flat rocky platform remains

[/FLOW]

Wave-cut platforms generally become wider as cliffs retreat.

### Caves, Arches and Stacks

[TABLE]
Stage | Process
Cave | Weakness/joint at headland enlarged by wave erosion.
Arch | Cave cuts through a headland.
Stack | Roof of arch collapses, leaving isolated rock pillar.
Stump | Further erosion lowers the stack.
[END TABLE]

### Depositional Coastal Landforms

### Beach

A beach is an accumulation of sand, gravel, pebbles or shell fragments along the shoreline.

### Spit

A spit is a narrow elongated depositional feature extending from the coastline into a bay, estuary or other body of water.

[FLOW]

Longshore drift → sediment transported along coast → deposition beyond coastline change → spit grows

[/FLOW]

### Barrier Island

A barrier island is an elongated sandbar parallel to the coast, often separating a lagoon or protected water body from the open sea.

### Tombolo

A tombolo is a depositional sand/gravel feature connecting an island to the mainland or another island.

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: Spit = sticks out | Tombolo = ties two landmasses | Barrier = blocks/protects the coast

[/EXAM FOCUS]

### Lagoon

A lagoon is a shallow water body separated from the open sea by a barrier island, sand bar, spit or reef structure.

Indian examples: Chilika, Pulicat and Vembanad.

### Estuary

An estuary is a partly enclosed coastal water body where freshwater from rivers mixes with seawater.

Estuaries are influenced strongly by tides, salinity gradients and sediment exchange.

### Mudflats and Salt Marshes

* Mudflats: flat areas of fine sediment exposed during low tide and submerged during high tide.

* Salt marshes: coastal wetlands dominated by salt-tolerant vegetation, especially in temperate regions.

### Coastal Erosion vs Deposition

[TABLE]
Dominant process | Typical features
Erosion | Cliff, sea cave, arch, stack, wave-cut platform.
Deposition | Beach, spit, bar, tombolo, barrier island, lagoon, mudflat, salt marsh.
[END TABLE]

## Glacial Processes and Landforms
-------------------------------------------------------

Glaciers are moving masses of ice that flow downslope under gravity.

They presently cover roughly 10% of Earth's land area, while during the Pleistocene their extent was much greater.

### Formation of Glacial Ice

[FLOW]

Snow accumulation > annual melting → compaction → granular firn/nevé → increasing pressure → dense glacial ice → glacier

[/FLOW]

Snowline is the approximate boundary above which snow remains year-round. It varies with latitude, temperature, precipitation and aspect.

### Glacial Movement

Glaciers move because of gravity through a combination of basal sliding and internal deformation.

[FLOW]

Gravity → basal sliding + internal deformation → glacier movement → erosion + transportation + deposition

[/FLOW]

### Types of Glacier Movement

* Basal sliding: glacier slides over underlying bedrock.

* Internal deformation: ice deforms and flows under stress.

* Compression and extension: ice speeds up/slows down and develops crevasses.

* Glacial surges: temporary periods of unusually rapid glacier advance.

### Glacial Erosion

[TABLE]
Process | Meaning
Plucking | Ice freezes onto bedrock blocks and pulls them away as the glacier moves.
Abrasion | Rock fragments embedded in basal ice scrape and grind the bedrock.
Freeze-thaw | Water freezes in cracks near glacial margins and weakens rock.
Polishing | Fine debris smooths and polishes bedrock surfaces.
[END TABLE]

[EXAM FOCUS]

Mnemonic: PAPF → Plucking + Abrasion + Polishing + Freeze-thaw

[/EXAM FOCUS]

-------------------------------------------------------
## Erosional Landforms of Glaciers
-------------------------------------------------------

### U-Shaped Valley

[FLOW]

River valley (V-shaped) → glacier occupies valley → strong lateral + basal erosion → wide floor + steep sides → U-shaped glacial valley

[/FLOW]

### Hanging Valley

A tributary glacial valley is left at a higher elevation than the main glacial valley because the main glacier erodes more deeply.

This can produce waterfalls where the tributary valley meets the main valley.

### Cirque / Corrie / Cwm

A cirque is an amphitheatre-like, bowl-shaped depression at the head of a glacial valley.

Important parts include the backwall, basin and threshold/sill.

### Arête

An arête is a sharp ridge separating two glacial valleys or cirques.

[FLOW]

Glaciers erode on both sides of a ridge → ridge becomes narrower → sharp knife-edge ridge → arête

[/FLOW]

### Horn / Pyramidal Peak

[FLOW]

Multiple cirques around a mountain → erosion from several sides → pyramidal peak → glacial horn

[/FLOW]

Example: Matterhorn, Alps.

### Truncated Spur

Glacial erosion truncates the projecting spurs that previously extended into a river valley.

### Ribbon Lake

Long, narrow lakes may develop in overdeepened parts of glacial valleys.

### Crag and Tail

A resistant rock obstacle creates a steep crag upstream and a gentler elongated tail downstream where glacial erosion and deposition interact.

### Fjord

A fjord is a deep, narrow, steep-sided glacial valley that has been submerged by the sea.

[FLOW]

Mountain glacier → U-shaped valley → glacial overdeepening → sea-level rise/land subsidence → seawater floods valley → fjord

[/FLOW]

Examples: Norway, New Zealand, Chile and Alaska.

[EXAM FOCUS]

UPSC 2023 Mains: UPSC asked directly about fjord formation and why fjords are among the most picturesque regions. The answer should connect glacial erosion + U-shaped valley + overdeepening + marine submergence.

[/EXAM FOCUS]

## Depositional Landforms of Glaciers
-------------------------------------------------------

Glaciers transport sediment ranging from clay-sized particles to huge boulders. When ice melts, the transported material is deposited as till.

### Moraines

[TABLE]
Type | Location
Lateral moraine | Along the sides of a glacier.
Medial moraine | At the junction of two glaciers, formed by joining lateral moraines.
Terminal moraine | At the glacier's maximum forward extent.
Ground moraine | Till spread beneath or behind retreating glacier ice.
[END TABLE]

[FLOW]

Two glaciers meet → lateral moraines merge → medial moraine forms

[/FLOW]

### Drumlins

Drumlins are streamlined, elongated hills composed largely of glacial till.

Their exact origin remains debated, but they are strongly associated with former glacial ice movement.

### Eskers

Eskers are long, sinuous ridges of sand and gravel deposited by meltwater streams flowing beneath or within glaciers.

### Outwash Plains

Outwash plains develop beyond terminal moraines where meltwater spreads sediment in front of the glacier.

[FLOW]

Glacier melts → meltwater flows outward → carrying capacity decreases → sand/gravel deposited → outwash plain

[/FLOW]

### Kettles and Kames

* Kettle: depression produced by melting of buried/isolated ice blocks in glacial deposits.

* Kame: mound or irregular deposit of sand/gravel formed by meltwater around stagnant/retreating ice.

## Human Impact on Exogenetic Processes
-------------------------------------------------------

Human activities can significantly change the rate and direction of erosion, weathering, runoff and mass wasting.

[TABLE]
Human activity | Geomorphic effect
Deforestation | Removes root binding and increases runoff and soil erosion.
Agriculture | Overgrazing, excessive tillage and farming on steep slopes can increase erosion.
Urbanisation | Changes drainage, increases impervious surfaces and can accelerate runoff.
Mining/quarrying | Removes protective cover and creates unstable slopes.
Road cutting | Steepens and weakens slopes, increasing landslide risk.
Dams/reservoirs | Trap sediment upstream and alter downstream sediment flow and channel morphology.
Climate change | Can alter rainfall intensity, glacier mass balance, permafrost stability and coastal erosion.
[END TABLE]

### Measures to Control Erosion and Slope Instability

* Afforestation and vegetation restoration

* Contour farming and contour bunding

* Terracing on steep agricultural slopes

* Check dams and gully plugs

* Riparian vegetation buffers

* Controlled drainage

* Slope stabilisation and bio-engineering

* Land-use zoning in landslide-prone regions

* Sediment-control measures around construction and mining sites

[FLOW]

Identify hazard → map vulnerable slopes/coasts/rivers → regulate land use → strengthen natural barriers → monitor → early warning → mitigation

[/FLOW]

## Common Prelims Traps
-------------------------------------------------------

[TABLE]
Confusion | Correct concept
Weathering transports material. | Weathering is essentially in situ.
Erosion means only wearing away. | Erosion involves detachment/removal; transportation is the subsequent movement.
Mass wasting requires flowing water. | Its primary controlling force is gravity; water may act as an important trigger.
Weathering is strongest everywhere in humid regions. | Type varies with climate; chemical weathering is favoured by warm, humid conditions.
Mechanical weathering changes minerals chemically. | Mechanical weathering mainly changes size and shape.
Carbonation is oxidation. | Carbonation involves carbonic acid and dissolution, especially of limestone.
Hydrolysis means water simply dissolves rock. | Hydrolysis involves a chemical reaction of water with minerals.
Attrition and abrasion are identical. | Abrasion = sediment wears the surface; attrition = particles collide with each other.
Creep is rapid. | Creep is extremely slow.
Slump is the same as any landslide. | Slump is a particular rotational slide along a curved surface.
Waterfall always forms because of hard rock only. | Differential erosion is important, but faulting, uplift and rejuvenation can also create waterfalls.
Meander erosion is strongest on the inner bank. | Outer/concave bank = erosion; inner/convex bank = deposition.
Oxbow lake is formed by deposition only. | It results from erosion of the meander neck + eventual cut-off + deposition/sealing.
Alluvial fan forms on very steep mountain slopes. | It forms where a stream emerges from a confined mountain valley onto a flatter area.
Natural levees are artificially built embankments. | They are natural depositional ridges formed along river banks.
All rivers form deltas. | Delta formation depends on sediment supply + wave/tide action + coastal conditions.
Estuary and delta are the same. | Estuary = drowned/marine-influenced river mouth; delta = sedimentary accumulation.
Groundwater caves form primarily by physical erosion. | Karst caves are strongly associated with chemical dissolution.
Stalagmites hang from the roof. | Stalactites hang from the roof; stalagmites grow from the floor.
Wind erosion is equally effective in humid forests. | Wind is especially important where vegetation is sparse, particularly arid/semi-arid environments.
Barchan horns point upwind. | Barchan horns point downwind.
Parabolic horns point downwind. | Parabolic horns point upwind.
Longitudinal dunes lie perpendicular to wind. | They are broadly parallel to the prevailing wind.
Loess is coarse sand. | Loess consists predominantly of fine wind-blown silt.
Stack forms directly from a cliff. | Common sequence: cave → arch → stack → stump.
Tombolo and spit are identical. | Spit projects into water; tombolo connects land to an island/another landmass.
Fjord is a river-made drowned valley. | Fjord = glacially carved U-shaped valley later flooded by the sea.
Glacier moves only by sliding. | Movement occurs through basal sliding + internal deformation.
Plucking is the same as abrasion. | Plucking removes blocks; abrasion grinds/scrapes bedrock.
Medial moraine occurs at the glacier's outer edge. | Medial moraine forms where two glaciers join, through merging lateral moraines.
Drumlin is a depositional coastal feature. | Drumlin is a glacial landform.
Esker is a mountain ridge formed by glacier ice itself. | Eskers are mainly meltwater-deposited ridges of sand and gravel.
[END TABLE]
