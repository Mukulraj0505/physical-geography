Chapter 9 — Plate Tectonics

Physical Geography • UPSC CSE Prelims + Mains

Plate Tectonics: The Unifying Theory

Plate tectonics explains the large-scale movement of Earth's lithosphere and the resulting distribution of continents, oceans, mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes, trenches and oceanic ridges.

The lithosphere is divided into a number of rigid plates that move over the weaker, ductile asthenosphere.

Earth's internal heat → mantle dynamics → plate movement → interaction at plate boundaries → mountains + rifts + trenches + volcanoes + earthquakes + ocean basins

Earth's continents cover roughly 29% of the surface, while oceans cover about 71%.

Core idea: It is not the continents alone that “float and move”. The entire lithospheric plate moves, carrying continental and/or oceanic crust with it.

Continental Drift Theory — Alfred Wegener

Alfred Wegener proposed the Continental Drift hypothesis in 1912 and developed it further in his later work. He argued that the present continents were once assembled into a single supercontinent.

Pangaea + Panthalassa → breakup → Laurasia + Gondwanaland → further fragmentation → present continents
Term Meaning
Pangaea Supercontinent containing almost all present continental landmasses.
Panthalassa Vast ocean surrounding Pangaea.
Laurasia Northern continental assemblage that included much of present North America, Europe and Asia.
Gondwanaland / Gondwana Southern continental assemblage including India, Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia and Madagascar.
Tethys Sea Oceanic realm between Laurasia and Gondwanaland in the classical reconstruction.
Pangaea → Laurasia + Gondwanaland → Atlantic expands + Indian Plate moves north → collision with Eurasia → Himalayas

Evidence for Continental Drift

Wegener supported continental drift using geological, palaeontological, climatic and geomorphological evidence.

Evidence Example Why significant
Jigsaw fit East coast of South America and west coast of Africa. The continental margins fit much better when continental shelves are considered rather than only present coastlines.
Matching rocks Ancient rock belts of Brazil correspond with rocks in western Africa. Similar age, composition and structure suggest a formerly continuous geological belt.
Placer deposits Gold deposits of Ghana can be linked to source rocks in Brazil. Suggests Brazil and West Africa were once adjacent.
Tillites / glacial deposits Gondwana tillites found in India and other Southern Hemisphere landmasses. Former widespread glaciation is easier to explain when these landmasses were assembled together near the South Pole.
Fossils Glossopteris and Mesosaurus occur on now-separated continents. Freshwater/land organisms could not easily cross large oceans, indicating former continental connection.
Palaeoclimatic evidence Coal deposits in now-cold regions and glacial deposits in present tropical regions. Indicates continents occupied different latitudinal positions in the past.
2025 UPSC Prelims: UPSC directly tested continental-drift evidence using Brazil–West Africa ancient rocks, Ghana gold deposits and Gondwana sediments. All three were treated as evidence.

Why Wegener's Theory Was Initially Rejected

The evidence for continental movement was significant, but Wegener could not provide a satisfactory physical mechanism capable of moving continents through the oceanic crust.

He proposed:

  • Pole-fleeing force associated with Earth's rotation.
  • Tidal force associated mainly with the gravitational influence of the Moon and Sun.
Correction: These proposed forces were considered far too weak to account for continental movement. Modern plate tectonics does not use Wegener's pole-fleeing and tidal forces as the principal driving mechanisms.

From Continental Drift to Plate Tectonics

Feature Continental Drift Sea-Floor Spreading Plate Tectonics
Main scientist(s) Alfred Wegener Harry Hess McKenzie, Morgan, Parker and others
Main idea Continents moved. New oceanic crust forms at ridges and moves outward. Rigid lithospheric plates move and interact.

Convection Currents and Driving Mechanisms

Arthur Holmes' Convection Current Theory

Arthur Holmes proposed that heat from Earth's interior can drive convection within the mantle.

Radioactive/primordial heat → mantle temperature differences → hot material rises → cooler material sinks → convection → contributes to plate movement

The classical model helped provide a mechanism missing from Wegener's theory, but mantle convection alone is not considered the complete explanation for plate motion.

Important modern view: Plate motion is influenced by a combination of forces, especially slab pull and ridge push, along with mantle flow and other interactions. Mantle convection should not be treated as a single simple conveyor belt that independently drives every plate.

Major Forces Driving Plate Motion

Force How it works
Slab pull Cold, dense oceanic lithosphere sinks into the mantle at subduction zones and pulls the rest of the plate behind it. It is widely regarded as one of the strongest contributors to plate motion.
Ridge push Elevated mid-ocean ridges create a gravitational tendency for lithosphere to move away from the ridge.
Basal drag / mantle flow Interaction between flowing mantle and the base of plates can contribute to plate motion.
Slab suction / trench-related forces Flow around subducting slabs can influence nearby plate motion.
Mnemonic: SRB → Slab Pull + Ridge Push + Basal/Mantle interactions

Sea-Floor Spreading — Harry Hess

Harry Hess proposed the sea-floor spreading hypothesis in the early 1960s. According to it, new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and moves away from the ridge on both sides.

Mantle upwelling → magma reaches mid-ocean ridge → cooling + solidification → new basaltic oceanic crust → crust moves away from ridge
New crust at ridge → outward movement → older oceanic crust moves farther away → old crust eventually subducted at trenches

This produces a continuous cycle of creation at divergent boundaries and destruction/recycling at convergent boundaries.

Evidence for Sea-Floor Spreading

Evidence Significance
Mid-ocean ridges Sites of magma upwelling and formation of new oceanic crust.
High heat flow Especially high near ridges because of hot material rising from below.
Young oceanic crust Crust becomes progressively older away from ridge axes; no extremely ancient oceanic crust survives because it is recycled.
Thin sediments near ridges Young crust has had less time to accumulate sediment.
Symmetrical magnetic stripes Alternating normal and reversed magnetic polarity occurs symmetrically on both sides of ridges.
Earthquake and volcanic activity Concentrated around active ridge systems.
Ocean-floor age pattern Provides direct evidence of outward movement from ridge axes.
Earth's magnetic field reverses → new basalt cools → magnetic minerals record polarity → seafloor spreads → alternating magnetic stripes → symmetrical pattern → evidence for spreading
Prelims core: Youngest oceanic crust = near ridge axis. Older oceanic crust is progressively farther from the ridge and is ultimately recycled at subduction zones.

Mid-Ocean Ridges

Mid-ocean ridges are enormous underwater mountain systems extending through the world's ocean basins.

  • Mid-Atlantic Ridge — classic divergent boundary.
  • East Pacific Rise — relatively rapid-spreading ridge.
  • Many ridges have a central rift valley, especially where spreading is relatively slow.
  • Associated with earthquakes, volcanism, high heat flow and hydrothermal activity.

Hydrothermal Vents

Hydrothermal vents occur where seawater circulates through hot oceanic crust, becomes heated and chemically enriched, and emerges back onto the seafloor.

Seawater enters cracks → heated by hot crust/magma → reacts with minerals → rises carrying dissolved chemicals → emerges as hydrothermal vent

Black smokers release hot, mineral-rich fluids and support unusual ecosystems based on chemosynthesis.

Lost City Hydrothermal Field on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is an important example.

Plate Tectonic Theory

The modern theory developed during the 1960s by combining continental drift, seafloor spreading, palaeomagnetism, ocean-floor mapping and the discovery of subduction zones.

Rigid lithosphere → divided into plates → plates move over weaker asthenosphere → interaction at boundaries → major geological activity

Plates vary enormously in size and may contain:

  • Mostly oceanic lithosphere — e.g. Pacific Plate.
  • Mostly continental lithosphere — parts of Eurasian Plate.
  • Both continental and oceanic lithosphere — many major plates.
Plate-count correction: The number of “major” plates varies according to classification. For UPSC, remember the conventional 7 major-plate framework plus numerous smaller plates, rather than treating “more than 50 plates” as a fixed universal number.

Major Plates

Major Plate Important geographical association
PacificLargest predominantly oceanic plate.
North AmericanNorth America and adjoining oceanic regions.

EurasianEurope and much of Asia.

Important smaller plates: Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Arabian, Philippine Sea, Juan de Fuca, Scotia, Caroline and Somali plates.

Map fact: The Pacific Plate is mainly oceanic, while the Indian Plate includes both continental and oceanic lithosphere.

Types of Plate Boundaries

Plate boundaries → Divergent + Convergent + Transform
Boundary Movement Crustal effect Major features
Divergent / Constructive Plates move apart. New crust formed. Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, volcanism, shallow earthquakes.
Convergent / Destructive Plates move towards each other. Crust may be subducted or strongly deformed. Trenches, volcanic arcs, fold mountains, deep earthquakes.
Transform / Conservative Plates slide horizontally past each other. No significant net creation/destruction of crust. Transform faults, earthquakes, offset landforms.

Divergent Plate Boundaries

Ocean–Ocean Divergence

Two oceanic plates move apart → asthenosphere upwells → decompression melting → magma rises → new oceanic crust → mid-ocean ridge

Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

The East Pacific Rise is another major oceanic spreading centre.

Continent–Continent Rifting

Continental lithosphere stretches → normal faulting → crust thins → rift valley → volcanism → continued extension → possible new ocean basin

Examples: East African Rift, Red Sea and Rhine Graben.

East African Rift System

The East African Rift is an active continental-rifting system involving the separation of the Nubian and Somali plates.

Continental extension → faulting + crustal thinning → volcanism + earthquakes → rift valleys + lakes → continued opening → possible future ocean

The Afar Triple Junction is a major tectonic junction involving the African/Nubian, Somali and Arabian plates.

Correction: The Indian Plate is not one of the three plates at the Afar Triple Junction. The important plates are the Nubian, Somali and Arabian plates.

Key associated features: Great Rift Valley, Lake Tanganyika, Lake Malawi, volcanic activity and extensive faulting.

Rhine Graben

The Upper Rhine Graben is a rift structure in western/central Europe associated with continental extension.

Convergent Plate Boundaries

Two plates move towards each other → collision/subduction → crustal deformation + earthquakes + volcanism + mountain/trench formation

Ocean–Continent Convergence

Dense oceanic plate → subducts beneath continental plate → trench → slab descends → partial melting at depth → magma rises → volcanic arc + mountain building

Example: Nazca Plate → subducts beneath South American Plate → Peru–Chile Trench + Andes.

Associated features include:

  • Deep-ocean trench
  • Accretionary prism
  • Forearc basin
  • Volcanic arc
  • Fold mountains
  • Intermediate and deep earthquakes

Ocean–Ocean Convergence

Two oceanic plates converge → older/denser plate subducts → trench → magma generation → volcanic island arc → earthquakes

Examples: Mariana arc, Japan arc, Aleutian arc and parts of the Indonesian island-arc system.

Examples of trenches: Mariana Trench, Tonga Trench, Japan Trench.

Continent–Continent Convergence

Two buoyant continental masses converge → neither easily subducts completely → intense compression → crustal shortening + thickening → uplift → fold mountains + plateau

Example: Indian Plate + Eurasian Plate → Himalayas + Tibetan Plateau.

Important: Continental–continental collision does not normally generate a classic oceanic subduction volcanic arc like the Andes. The Himalaya is therefore primarily a collision mountain system, not a volcanic arc.

Transform Plate Boundaries

At a transform boundary, plates move horizontally past one another.

Plate A ⇄ Plate B → shear stress → faulting → earthquakes

Examples:

  • San Andreas Fault — California.
  • Hayward Fault — California.
  • Alpine Fault — New Zealand.

Transform boundaries generally produce abundant earthquakes but do not normally create major volcanic arcs.

Horizontal displacement can produce:

  • Offset rivers and drainage channels
  • Linear valleys
  • Fault scarps
  • Pull-apart basins

Hotspots and Mantle Plumes

A hotspot is a region of persistent or long-lived volcanic activity that may occur away from a plate boundary.

Hot mantle upwelling → partial melting → magma reaches crust → hotspot volcanism

A mantle plume is a proposed column of anomalously hot, buoyant mantle rising from deep within the mantle.

Mantle plume → hot material rises → partial melting → hotspot → volcano

Hotspot track

Hotspot remains approximately stationary relative to moving plate → plate moves over hotspot → new volcano forms → older volcano carried away → volcanic chain develops

Hawaiian Islands provide the classic example.

  • Youngest active volcanoes occur near the present hotspot area.
  • Volcanoes become progressively older away from the active hotspot.
  • The chain records the direction and broad rate of Pacific Plate movement.

Important examples:

Hotspot / volcanic system Example
Hawaiian hotspot Hawaiian island chain in the Pacific.
Réunion hotspot Associated in the hotspot/plume model with the Deccan flood-basalt province.
Galápagos Intraplate hotspot volcanism modified by a complex plate setting.
Yellowstone Hotspot-related volcanic system beneath western United States.
Important qualification: The simple idea that every hotspot is produced by a single deep, perfectly stationary mantle plume is an idealised model. Some hotspots may have shallower or more complex origins, and hotspot motion is also possible.
2018 UPSC Mains: UPSC directly asked “Define mantle plume and explain its role in plate tectonics.”

Trenches and Ocean Basins

Ocean trenches are long, narrow, deep depressions associated mainly with subduction zones.

Mariana Trench in the western Pacific contains the Challenger Deep, the deepest known point on Earth's oceans at roughly 11 km below sea level.

Mnemonic: Trench = Subduction
Think: Dense oceanic plate → Down → Deep trench

Wilson Cycle: Opening and Closing of Ocean Basins

The Wilson Cycle describes the cyclic opening, widening, narrowing and eventual closure of ocean basins through plate tectonic processes.

Embryonic stage → Young/Juvenile stage → Mature ocean → Declining ocean → Terminal ocean → Suturing → New continental rifting
Stage Main process Example / feature
Embryonic Continental rifting begins. Early continental rift; East African Rift.
Juvenile Rift develops into a narrow ocean. Red Sea.
Mature Well-developed ocean basin with passive margins. Atlantic Ocean.
Declining Subduction starts consuming oceanic crust. Ocean begins to contract.
Terminal Ocean becomes very narrow as continents approach collision. Tethys-type evolutionary stage.
Suturing Continents collide and ocean closes. Mountain belt forms.
Continental rift → narrow sea → mature ocean → subduction → shrinking ocean → continental collision → mountain belt

Pamir Knot

The Pamir Knot is a major mountain junction in Central Asia produced by the broad tectonic convergence associated with the Indian and Eurasian plates.

The region links major mountain systems including the Pamirs, Hindu Kush, Karakoram, Kunlun and Tien Shan.

Indian Plate convergence → crustal shortening/thickening → uplift → Himalaya + Tibetan Plateau + Central Asian mountain systems

The Pamir region remains tectonically active and is prone to earthquakes and associated hazards.

Distribution of Earthquakes and Volcanoes

Plate boundaries → concentrated tectonic stress + magma generation → global belts of earthquakes and volcanoes

Circum-Pacific Belt / Ring of Fire

The Ring of Fire encircles much of the Pacific Ocean and contains a very high concentration of active volcanoes and earthquakes.

Pacific plate margins → extensive subduction → trenches + volcanic arcs + deep earthquakes → Circum-Pacific Belt

Important regions include:

  • Andes
  • Central America
  • Cascades
  • Alaska and Aleutian arc
  • Japan
  • Philippines
  • Indonesia
  • New Zealand
2020 UPSC Mains: UPSC asked about the geophysical characteristics of the Circum-Pacific Zone. Prepare it through subduction + trenches + island/continental volcanic arcs + earthquakes + mountain building.

Volcanic Island Arcs

Volcanic island arcs generally develop where an oceanic plate subducts beneath another oceanic plate.

Oceanic–oceanic convergence → subduction → mantle melting → magma rises → volcanoes → curved volcanic island arc

Examples: Aleutian Islands, Japanese Arc, Philippine Arc, Indonesian Arc and Lesser Antilles.

Island arcs are commonly associated with deep ocean trenches, earthquakes and back-arc basins.

Why Plate Tectonics Changes Continents and Oceans

Divergence → new oceanic crust → ocean basin expands
Convergence + subduction → oceanic crust consumed → ocean basin shrinks
Continental collision → crust thickens → mountains + plateaus
Transform motion → lateral displacement → faults + earthquakes
Rifting → crust thins → continental breakup → new ocean may form

Thus, the shape and position of continents and ocean basins are dynamic rather than permanent.

2025 UPSC Mains: UPSC directly asked how tectonic movements reshape the configuration of continents and ocean basins. This is a major answer-writing theme.

Significance of Plate Tectonics

Dimension Impact
Geomorphology Explains mountain ranges, plateaus, ocean ridges, trenches and rift valleys.
Earthquakes Explains the global concentration of seismicity along plate boundaries.
Volcanism Explains volcanic arcs, mid-ocean-ridge volcanism and many hotspot systems.
Climate Changing continental positions alter ocean currents, atmospheric circulation, rainfall and long-term climate.
Biogeography Continental separation creates isolation, migration pathways and evolutionary divergence.
Resources Tectonic activity contributes to mineralisation, hydrothermal deposits, geothermal resources and sedimentary basin development.
Water resources Mountain building and tectonic relief strongly influence river systems, glaciers and groundwater basins.
Disaster management Understanding tectonic settings helps assess earthquake, tsunami and volcanic hazards.
