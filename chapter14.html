Chapter 14 — Insolation & Heat Balance

Energy, Heat and Temperature

Energy is the capacity to do work. It exists in many forms such as kinetic, potential, electrical, chemical and thermal energy. Energy can be transformed from one form to another, but cannot be created or destroyed according to the law of conservation of energy.

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.

Heat is energy transferred between bodies because of a temperature difference.

Heat Temperature
Energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Measure of the average kinetic energy of particles.
Measured in joules, calories, etc. Measured in °C, °F or K.
Depends on amount of substance as well as temperature. Does not directly represent total thermal energy.
Remember: Heat flows naturally from a higher-temperature body → lower-temperature body.

Heat Transfer

Heat is transferred through conduction, convection and radiation. Advection is also important in geographical heat transport.

Mechanism Meaning Example
Conduction Transfer of heat through direct molecular contact. Ground warming the air immediately above it.
Convection Vertical transfer of heat through movement of a fluid. Warm air rising and cool air sinking.
Advection Horizontal transfer of heat by moving air or water. Warm ocean current transporting heat poleward.
Radiation Transfer of energy through electromagnetic waves; no material medium is required. Solar energy reaching Earth.

Mnemonic: CCR + A

Conduction = Contact
Convection = Vertical
Radiation = Rays
Advection = Across/Horizontally

Sensible and Latent Heat

Sensible heat: Heat transfer that changes temperature without changing the physical state of a substance.

Latent heat: Energy absorbed or released during a change of state without a corresponding change in temperature.

Evaporation → absorbs latent heat
Condensation → releases latent heat
Atmospheric importance: Evaporation stores energy as latent heat; condensation releases that energy and contributes strongly to atmospheric convection and storm development.

Earth–Sun Relationship

Earth receives energy from the Sun primarily in the form of electromagnetic radiation. This incoming energy drives Earth's climate system, weather, hydrological cycle and biological processes.

Earth's Axial Tilt

  • Earth's axis is tilted by approximately 23.44° from the perpendicular to the plane of its orbit.
  • Equivalently, the Earth's axis makes an angle of about 66.56° with the plane of the ecliptic.
  • The axial tilt is a major cause of seasons and changing solar altitude/day length.

Mnemonic: 23.5° = Tilt → Seasons.

Earth's Rotation and Revolution

Movement Main significance
Rotation Causes day and night and produces apparent daily movement of the Sun.
Revolution Earth's annual orbital movement, combined with axial tilt, produces seasonal changes.

Earth completes one revolution relative to the seasonal cycle in approximately 365.24 days.

The sidereal year is approximately 365.256 days, whereas the tropical year is approximately 365.242 days.

Perihelion and Aphelion

  • Perihelion: Earth is closest to the Sun, around early January.
  • Aphelion: Earth is farthest from the Sun, around early July.
  • Because of the elliptical orbit, incoming solar energy varies over the year.
  • However, seasons are primarily caused by axial tilt, not Earth–Sun distance.

The solar-energy difference between perihelion and aphelion is significant but secondary compared with the effect of axial tilt on seasonal insolation.

Classic UPSC trap: Northern Hemisphere summer does not occur because Earth is closest to the Sun. In fact, Earth is near aphelion during Northern Hemisphere summer.

Insolation

Insolation = Incoming Solar Radiation received by Earth.

Most incoming solar radiation is in relatively short wavelengths.

Solar radiation includes ultraviolet, visible and infrared components, with a large proportion concentrated in the visible and near-infrared portions of the spectrum.

Solar Constant

The solar constant is the mean solar irradiance received at the top of Earth's atmosphere on a surface perpendicular to the Sun's rays at the mean Earth–Sun distance.

Approximate value: 1361 W/m².

Correction: Older textbooks frequently use ~1367 W/m². The currently accepted approximate total solar irradiance is about 1361 W/m².

Factors Affecting Insolation

The amount of insolation received at a place depends mainly on:

Factor Effect
Latitude Solar angle generally becomes lower with increasing latitude, reducing energy received per unit area.
Angle of incidence Near-perpendicular rays concentrate energy on a smaller area; oblique rays spread the same energy over a larger area.
Duration of sunshine Longer daylight generally allows greater daily receipt of solar energy.
Earth–Sun distance Insolation is slightly greater near perihelion and lower near aphelion.
Atmospheric transparency Clouds, dust, gases and aerosols may absorb, scatter or reflect solar radiation.
Surface characteristics Albedo determines the fraction reflected rather than absorbed.

Mnemonic: LADASA

Latitude + Angle + Duration + Air/Atmosphere + Sun distance + Albedo.

Angle of Incidence

When solar rays strike the surface nearly vertically, energy is concentrated over a smaller area and the intensity of insolation is higher.

When rays are oblique, the same energy is spread over a larger surface area and passes through a greater thickness of atmosphere.

Vertical rays → smaller area → greater intensity
Oblique rays → larger area + longer atmospheric path → lower intensity

Why Does the Equator Not Always Have the Highest Surface Temperature?

The equatorial region receives strong solar radiation, but maximum surface temperatures are often found in the subtropical desert belts rather than exactly at the Equator.

Major reasons include:

  • Persistent equatorial cloud cover reflects part of incoming radiation.
  • Strong convection transports heat upward.
  • Heavy rainfall and evaporation consume substantial energy.
  • Subtropical high-pressure belts have generally clearer skies and descending dry air.
Concept: Maximum insolation, maximum surface temperature and maximum mean annual temperature need not occur at exactly the same latitude.

Seasons

The changing seasons are primarily caused by:

Earth's axial tilt + revolution around the Sun → changing solar angle + changing day length → seasons

Not the cause: changing Earth–Sun distance is not the principal reason for seasons.

Mnemonic:

MARCH = Middle (Equator) JUNE = Cancer SEPTEMBER = Middle (Equator) DECEMBER = Capricorn

Season mnemonic for Northern Hemisphere:

J → Summer → North tilted toward Sun D → Winter → North tilted away from Sun

Solstice Logic

  • During the June solstice, Northern Hemisphere receives more direct rays and has longer days.
  • During the December solstice, Southern Hemisphere receives more direct rays and has longer days.
  • At the equinoxes, the Sun is overhead near the Equator and day/night are approximately equal worldwide.

Important: Day and night are not exactly 12 hours everywhere on the equinox because of atmospheric refraction and the finite angular size of the Sun.

Day Length

Poleward → seasonal variation in daylight increases

At the poles, summer may experience approximately 24-hour daylight, while winter may experience approximately 24-hour darkness.

Midnight Sun

The midnight sun is the phenomenon in which the Sun remains above the horizon at midnight.

It occurs within the Arctic Circle and Antarctic Circle during their respective summer periods.

June: Midnight sun in the Arctic region.
December: Midnight sun in the Antarctic region.

Heat Balance / Heat Budget of the Earth

The heat budget refers to the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing terrestrial radiation over sufficiently long periods.

Incoming solar radiation ≈ Outgoing terrestrial radiation → long-term thermal balance

Earth's climate system does not have an identical energy balance at every location. There are regional surpluses and deficits, which are redistributed by atmospheric circulation and ocean currents.

Classic 100-Unit Heat Budget

For UPSC revision, the classical NCERT-style budget can be remembered as follows:

Process Approx. units
Incoming solar radiation 100
Reflected back to space 35
Absorbed by Earth–atmosphere system 65
Absorbed by atmosphere/clouds 14
Absorbed by Earth's surface 51
Surface long-wave radiation absorbed by atmosphere 34
Surface radiation escaping directly to space 17
Atmosphere radiated to space 48

Check:

35 reflected + 65 retained/absorbed = 100
17 direct from surface + 48 from atmosphere = 65 outgoing

Heat Budget mnemonic: “35 – 65 / 14 – 51 / 34 – 17 / 48”

Think:

100 in → 35 reflected → 65 absorbed 65 = 14 atmosphere + 51 surface 51 = 34 absorbed by atmosphere + 17 escapes directly Atmosphere sends 48 to space
Important: These values are a classical teaching model used for conceptual understanding, not a statement that Earth's real energy budget consists of permanently fixed whole-number percentages.

Shortwave and Longwave Radiation

Feature Shortwave Radiation Longwave Radiation
Source Mainly Sun Mainly Earth and atmosphere
Wavelength Relatively short Relatively long
Nature Mainly UV, visible and near-infrared solar energy Terrestrial infrared radiation
Atmospheric interaction Atmosphere is comparatively transparent to much of it Strongly absorbed by greenhouse gases and clouds at particular wavelengths
Main role Provides incoming solar energy Earth's mechanism for losing heat to space

Memory trick:

SUN → SHORTWAVE IN EARTH → LONGWAVE OUT

Transmission, Absorption, Reflection and Scattering

Transmission

Transmission occurs when radiation passes through the atmosphere or another medium without being significantly absorbed or reflected.

Absorption

Atmospheric gases and clouds absorb particular wavelengths of radiation.

Important absorbers include:

  • Water vapour
  • Carbon dioxide
  • Ozone
  • Clouds and aerosols for particular portions of the spectrum

Reflection

Radiation is sent back without being absorbed.

Clouds, snow, ice and some land surfaces have relatively high reflectivity.

Scattering

Scattering redirects radiation in different directions because of interaction with atmospheric molecules and particles.

Rayleigh scattering: scattering by molecules much smaller than the wavelength of visible light; shorter wavelengths are scattered more strongly.

Blue/violet wavelengths → stronger scattering Red wavelengths → comparatively weaker scattering

Albedo

Albedo is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface.

Albedo = Reflected solar radiation / Incoming solar radiation
  • High albedo → more reflection → less absorption.
  • Low albedo → less reflection → greater absorption.
Surface General albedo tendency
Fresh snow Very high
Ice High to moderate, depending on condition
Desert/sandy surfaces Moderate to high
Vegetation Generally lower
Dark ocean surface Generally low, especially when the Sun is high

Mnemonic: WHITE → REFLECTS; DARK → ABSORBS.

Albedo and Climate

Ice melts → darker surface exposed → more solar absorption → warming → more melting

This is a positive feedback known as the ice–albedo feedback.

Atmospheric Windows

An atmospheric window is a wavelength region through which radiation can pass relatively efficiently through the atmosphere.

Radiation Atmospheric behaviour
Much visible sunlight Passes relatively efficiently through the atmosphere under clear conditions.
Some infrared wavelengths Relatively transparent and form important atmospheric windows.
Other infrared wavelengths Absorbed strongly by greenhouse gases and water vapour.

An important terrestrial infrared atmospheric window is approximately the 8–14 μm region, although atmospheric transmission varies with water vapour and other conditions.

Greenhouse Effect

The natural greenhouse effect is the process through which certain atmospheric gases absorb and re-emit part of Earth's outgoing long-wave radiation, helping keep the surface warmer than it would otherwise be.

Solar shortwave enters → surface absorbs → surface emits longwave → greenhouse gases absorb → re-radiation → warming of lower atmosphere/surface

Major Greenhouse Gases

Gas Important source/role
Water vapour Most important natural greenhouse gas; concentration responds strongly to temperature.
CO2 Fossil fuels, deforestation, cement production; major anthropogenic forcing agent.
CH4 Wetlands, livestock, rice cultivation, fossil-fuel systems and waste.
N2O Agriculture and industrial processes.
O3 Greenhouse effect varies by atmospheric layer; stratospheric ozone also absorbs UV radiation.
Do not confuse:
Natural greenhouse effect makes Earth habitable.
Enhanced greenhouse effect results from increased concentrations of greenhouse gases, contributing to modern global warming.

Why the Atmosphere is Heated Mainly from Below

The atmosphere absorbs only part of incoming solar radiation directly. A large fraction reaches Earth's surface, which absorbs the energy and emits long-wave terrestrial radiation.

Sun → shortwave → Earth surface → longwave → atmosphere → atmospheric heating

Additional heat reaches the atmosphere through convection, conduction, latent heat release and absorption of solar radiation.

UPSC Concept: In the lower atmosphere, the Earth's surface is the dominant immediate source of heating. Therefore, the troposphere is said to be heated from below.

Variation of Insolation Over Earth's Surface

Latitudinal Heat Balance

Because solar angle and day length vary by latitude, different regions receive different amounts of net radiation.

Low latitudes → greater annual radiation surplus Mid-latitudes → transition High latitudes → radiation deficit

In the classical geographical framework, a broad belt of net radiation surplus extends roughly from 40°N to 40°S, while higher latitudes generally experience a deficit.

This imbalance drives the need for poleward heat transport through:

  • Atmospheric circulation
  • Ocean currents
Equatorial/tropical surplus → atmospheric + oceanic transport → higher latitudes → global heat redistribution

Heat Surplus and Heat Deficit Regions

Heat Surplus

  • Generally found at low latitudes.
  • Solar radiation received exceeds energy lost over longer periods.
  • Strong evaporation and convection redistribute part of the surplus.

Heat Deficit

  • Generally found at high latitudes.
  • Low solar angles and seasonal darkness reduce incoming energy.
  • Energy loss can exceed incoming radiation.

Land and Water Heating

Land and water respond differently to incoming solar energy.

Land Water
Heats and cools relatively rapidly. Heats and cools relatively slowly.
Lower effective heat storage compared with deep water bodies. High heat capacity and mixing allow greater heat storage.
Large daily and annual temperature range. Smaller temperature range.
Strongly influences continental climates. Moderates coastal climates.

Mnemonic: Land = Fast; Water = Slow.

Seasonal Insolation and Surface Conditions

Dry vs Moist Surfaces

  • Dry conditions can permit strong daytime heating under clear skies.
  • Moist conditions support evaporation, transferring energy into latent heat.
  • Clouds can reduce incoming shortwave radiation but can also reduce nighttime cooling by absorbing/re-emitting longwave radiation.

Clouds and Heat Budget

Day Night
Clouds may reflect incoming solar radiation and produce a cooling tendency. Clouds absorb/re-emit outgoing longwave radiation and can reduce radiative cooling.
UPSC example: Dew formation is generally favoured on clear nights because the surface can lose heat rapidly through outgoing longwave radiation. Cloudy nights reduce this radiative cooling.

Aspect, Slope and Insolation

Aspect means the direction in which a slope faces.

In the Northern Hemisphere:

  • South-facing slopes generally receive more direct sunlight and tend to be warmer.
  • North-facing slopes generally receive less direct sunlight and tend to be cooler.

This can affect:

  • Snow persistence
  • Soil moisture
  • Vegetation
  • Agriculture
  • Local microclimates
Important: The reverse tendency generally applies in the Southern Hemisphere.

Greenhouse Effect vs Heat Budget

Greenhouse Effect Heat Budget
Mechanism of absorption and re-emission of longwave radiation by gases/clouds. Overall accounting of incoming and outgoing energy.
Helps determine Earth's surface temperature. Describes Earth's thermal equilibrium and regional energy surplus/deficit.
Main gases include H2O, CO2, CH4 etc. Includes reflection, absorption, terrestrial radiation and energy transfer.

Heat Islands and Urban Energy Balance

The Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect refers to the tendency of urban areas to be warmer than surrounding rural areas.

Major Causes

  • Concrete and asphalt absorb and store heat.
  • Reduced vegetation reduces evapotranspiration.
  • Buildings reduce wind flow and alter radiation exchange.
  • Anthropogenic heat from vehicles, buildings and industries adds energy.
  • Urban geometry can trap radiation.
Concrete/asphalt ↑ + vegetation ↓ + anthropogenic heat ↑ → urban heat storage ↑ → UHI

Mitigation: urban vegetation, reflective/cool roofs, permeable surfaces, improved ventilation corridors and reduced anthropogenic heat.

Heat Waves in India

A heat wave is a period of unusually hot weather whose significance depends on local climatic conditions and duration.

IMD Criteria — High-Yield Fact

Condition Criterion
Plains Heat-wave consideration begins when maximum temperature reaches at least 40°C.
Hilly regions Threshold is at least 30°C.
Departure from normal 4.5–6.4°C = Heat Wave; >6.4°C = Severe Heat Wave.
Actual maximum temperature ≥45°C = Heat Wave; ≥47°C = Severe Heat Wave.
Coastal areas Heat wave may be described when maximum temperature is at least 37°C and departure from normal is at least 4.5°C.

For the relevant absolute/departure criteria, IMD uses a combination of temperature thresholds and departures from normal; the operational declaration also involves spatial and persistence requirements.

Why Heat Waves Occur in India

  • Strong pre-monsoon solar heating.
  • Persistent clear skies.
  • Hot and dry continental air masses.
  • Subsidence under high-pressure conditions.
  • Hot winds from arid/semi-arid regions.
  • Dry soils reduce evaporative cooling.
  • Urbanisation amplifies local warming through the urban heat island effect.
Strong solar heating + dry air + clear sky + weak evaporative cooling → heat-wave conditions

Heat Wave Impacts

Sector Impact
Health Heat exhaustion, heat stroke, dehydration and increased mortality risk.
Agriculture Crop stress, yield losses, livestock stress and increased irrigation demand.
Water Higher evaporation and greater demand for water.
Energy Higher cooling demand and pressure on electricity systems.
Urban systems Higher indoor temperatures and greater exposure of vulnerable populations.

Heat-Action Approach

Early warning → Public awareness → Cooling measures → Health preparedness → Targeted support → Post-event assessment

Heat-health warning systems and Heat Action Plans are examples of adaptation measures used to reduce risks from extreme heat. WMO emphasises that heat-wave definitions depend on local climatic conditions rather than a single universal temperature threshold.

Climate Change and Earth's Energy Budget

Climate change can occur when factors alter the balance between energy received by Earth and energy emitted back to space.

Climate Forcing

A climate forcing is a factor that changes the Earth's radiative energy balance.

Natural forcing Human-related forcing
Changes in solar activity Increasing greenhouse-gas concentrations
Volcanic eruptions Anthropogenic aerosols
Orbital variations over very long timescales Land-use change

Positive and Negative Energy Imbalance

Incoming energy > outgoing energy → positive imbalance → warming
Outgoing energy > incoming energy → negative imbalance → cooling

Ice–Albedo Feedback

Warming → ice/snow melt → albedo decreases → more solar absorption → further warming

This is a classic example of a positive climate feedback.

Oceanic Role in Heat Redistribution

Oceans absorb and store enormous quantities of solar energy and redistribute heat through ocean currents.

Tropical surplus → warm currents → poleward heat transport

Cold currents transport relatively cooler water toward lower latitudes and can moderate coastal climates.

Atmospheric Circulation and Heat Redistribution

The unequal distribution of heating between low and high latitudes creates pressure and temperature differences that contribute to large-scale atmospheric circulation.

Unequal heating → pressure differences → winds → poleward/equatorward heat transport

Ocean currents provide another major mechanism for redistributing heat.

Core geographical relationship:
Heat imbalance → atmospheric circulation + ocean circulation → redistribution of heat → global climate system.
